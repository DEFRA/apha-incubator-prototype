Priority view design test

Ready to test

Part of round 1. The priority view is the top-level page of the early warning service. It ranks animal diseases (currently fictional) by priority, so people can see which need action first.

These notes are from the design team, to help the user researcher plan the research. They are not a test plan. The method, tasks, participants, session structure and analysis are for the researcher and the wider team to decide. All data is illustrative and the disease names are fictional. Last updated 12 November 2026.

The prototype

Priority view v1.1 was made after the team design review. It comes in two versions, so we can see how people react to preparedness shown as one measure and as two. The test leads with combined preparedness. The separate version is to show if combined preparedness is flagged as not working for people. Every other change is the same in both. Each version is one page with a switch between a table and cards, and the two pages link to each other. v1.0 was replaced by v1.1 before any testing and is kept for reference. If v1.1 is tweaked during the round, the changes become v1.2 and are listed on the round 1 page.

Which view people see first

The team has decided to lead with combined preparedness, and to show the separate version only if combined preparedness is flagged as not working for people. Whether people start on cards or the table, or alternate, is for the user researcher and the wider team to decide. Some things that may help:

  • Cards lead with a short reason for each disease and group the diseases by priority action. The table suits comparing one measure across diseases, and can be sorted.
  • The view people see first may shape what they do next. Alternating spreads that across participants, but needs enough people on each starting view.
  • Either view opens directly from a link, and the choice is kept in the address, so a link shares the view. The link between the two preparedness versions keeps the chosen view and sort.

Options A and B of the priority view have not had the design changes made to option C, so they are not planned for testing.

What we would like to learn

These are the design team’s questions. Use, change or replace them.

  • Can people tell which diseases need action first, and why?
  • Do they read the risk and impact levels, and the preparedness measures, the way we intend?
  • Do people prefer preparedness as one measure with a status, or as two (control measures and response options)? We lead with the combined version. What does it let them do, or stop them doing, and what makes them ask for the separate detail?
  • What change do people want to see surfaced, why, and what would they do with it? We removed the change display (see below), so this is a question to ask, not assume.
  • What would they want from risk, impact, control measures and response options pages? The priority view is a starting point for that conversation.
  • Which view do they use, do they switch, and why?
  • What do they expect to find behind each "More details" link?

What is made up

  • The scores and which disease has which risk, impact and preparedness. The previous change history has been removed, because it rested on too many assumptions. Diseases already active in the UK are not covered, so none is shown.
  • The model, which is a fictional process that considers all four measures: threat (risk of incursion 1 to 6 × impact 1 to 5, up to 30) multiplied by 1 plus a preparedness gap (the average share of control measure and response option items that need review). That gives a score out of 60, with cut-offs at 30, 20 and 8. The real scoring will be worked out later.
  • The checklist totals (10 control items and 8 response items), the thresholds that turn the gap into a preparedness status, and how often risk and impact are updated.

Before testing with users

Colour contrast was checked as we designed, and every pair passes 4.5:1 for text. Not checked yet: a screen reader pass, 200% zoom and text spacing, forced colours mode, and an automated contrast check on this version. We need an accessibility review before testing with users.

Design decisions

The design decisions page lists every decision behind v1.1 and v1.0, with the reason and whether it is decided, recommended or for the team to decide. Five decision pages compare the choices we looked at:

  • Arranging the priority cell
  • Showing control measures and response options as checklists
  • Bars under the risk and impact levels
  • Explaining the measures on the cards
  • Showing when a priority score changed
  • Signalling priority without prescribing action, to review

Open questions

  • How do we signal priority without prescribing action? The priority actions (Act now, Prioritise, Monitor, Maintain) were raised in the team design review as not helpful at an individual level, because not everyone needs to act. The CEO does need to organise action, so the column still has to get attention. Seven options are to review. v1.1 still uses the priority actions.
  • How should preparedness feed into priority? The prototype now considers all four measures in a fictional scoring process: threat (risk × impact) is raised by a preparedness gap, so a badly prepared disease scores higher than a well prepared one with the same risk and impact. The real scoring is for the model owners to work out. We need to know which of the four measures should carry the most weight, and whether preparedness should raise the score or set the priority action.
  • Should preparedness be one measure or two? The test leads with one (combined). The separate version is to show if combined is flagged as not working. People may need to tell prevention (control measures) from response, or may only need an overall status. How the five statuses are worked out from the underlying items is made up and needs the real method.
  • What change do people want surfaced? We removed the change dates, previous scores and tags, because they rested on too many assumptions. Research should ask what change people want to see, why, and what they would do with it, then design from there. Earlier thinking is in showing when a priority score changed.
  • Levels and checklists. The prototype shows risk of incursion as six levels (Negligible to Very high) and impact as five. Do these line up with the levels in the monthly report? Does impact change over time, or is it fixed until reassessed? The checklist totals (10 control items and 8 response items), how often each is updated and the priority cut-offs (30, 20 and 8 out of 60) are all assumed. We need the real ones.
  • Is there a "what to do now" page behind "Act now: see why this is a priority"? The wording promises the reason for the priority, not actions. If the disease page has actions, we can say so. If not, it stays as it is.
  • Should the Disease cell also carry a short summary? It fits (the card text at 16px) but makes the table about 20% taller. The name cell is the row header, so a screen reader would read the whole summary on each cell of the row. It is not added. The same is true of the More details link in that cell, which research should check.
  • Who writes the reason on each card, and how often? The prototype shows it as written copy. Does anyone also need a summary at the top of the page, what should it say, and does it need to differ by role?
  • How do people reach a measure page? The prototype has no "Look in more detail" line, so people rely on the top navigation. If they miss it, we try column headings as links.
  • Real or fictional disease names in the research? Fictional names avoid distraction, but experts may find it harder to judge whether the results are plausible.
  • Which participants, and how do we recruit people who work by disease and by measure?

Wider questions, such as brand and real data, are in the design log.
