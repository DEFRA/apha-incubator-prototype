Priority view design test

Ready to test

Part of round 1. The priority view is the top-level page of the early warning service. It ranks animal diseases (currently fictional) by priority, so people can see which need action first.

These notes are from the design team, to help the user researcher plan the research. They are not a test plan. The method, tasks, participants, session structure and analysis are for the researcher and the wider team to decide. All data is illustrative and the disease names are fictional. Last updated 12 November 2026.

The prototype

Priority view v1.0 is one page with a switch between a table and cards. It is a frozen copy, so it stays as it is while the design moves on. If it is tweaked during the round, the changes become v1.1 and are listed on the round 1 page.

Priority view prototype v1.0
Open What it is
Cards view Diseases grouped by priority action, each with a short reason and its four measures
Table view Every disease in one sortable table, for comparing one measure across diseases
Images: cards (image) and table (image) Long screenshots of each view, for sharing and viewing on a screen

Links that go beyond the priority view, such as a disease page or the risk page, lead to a short "this page is not part of the test" page that shows which route the person chose.

Which view people see first

This is for the user researcher and the wider team to decide: cards first, table first, or alternating between them. Some things that may help:

  • Cards lead with a short reason for each disease and group the diseases by priority action. The table suits comparing one measure across diseases, and can be sorted.
  • The view people see first may shape what they do next. Alternating spreads that across participants, but needs enough people on each starting view.
  • Either view opens directly from a link, and the choice is kept in the address, so a link shares the view.

Options A and B of the priority view have not had the design changes made to option C, so they are not planned for testing.

What we would like to learn

These are the design team’s questions. Use, change or replace them.

  • Can people tell which diseases need action first, and why?
  • Do they read the level names, the "need review" checklists and the "Changed" date the way we intend?
  • What would they want from risk, impact, control measures and response options pages? The priority view is a starting point for that conversation.
  • Which view do they use, do they switch, and why?
  • What do they expect to find behind each "More details" link?

What is made up

  • The scores, change dates and previous scores, and which disease has which impact areas.
  • The model: priority is risk × impact out of 25, with cut-offs at 15, 10 and 4. Control measures and response options do not change the score.
  • The checklist totals (10 control items and 8 response items), and how often risk and impact are updated.

Before testing with users

Colour contrast was checked as we designed, and every pair passes 4.5:1 for text. Not checked yet: a screen reader pass, 200% zoom and text spacing, forced colours mode, and an automated contrast check on this version. We need an accessibility review before testing with users.

Design decisions

The design decisions page lists every decision behind v1.0, with the reason and whether it is decided, recommended or for the team to decide. Five decision pages compare the choices we looked at:

  • Arranging the priority cell
  • Showing control measures and response options as checklists
  • Bars under the risk and impact levels
  • Explaining the measures on the cards
  • Showing when a priority score changed

Open questions

  • How does preparedness link into priority? The model is risk × impact only. Control measures and response options are checklists of items needing review, and they sit beside the priority score without changing it. So a well-prepared disease and a badly prepared one with the same risk and impact get the same priority. Is that what we want? Should the checklists lower or raise the score, set the priority action, or only inform what to do? This needs an answer from the model owners before we show how priority is worked out.
  • What does "Changed [date]" mean? The prototype shows the date a priority score last changed, on every row. Does it mean the score value changed, or that risk and impact were last checked, even if the score stayed the same? The second is probably better for trust, but harder to explain. Do we need a rule for how long a change tag stays, and do we have the dates and previous scores for every disease?
  • Levels and checklists. The prototype shows risk and impact as five named levels, which should line up with the five risk levels in the monthly report. Do they? Does impact change over time, or is it fixed until reassessed? The checklist totals (10 control items and 8 response items), how often each is updated and the priority cut-offs (15, 10 and 4) are all assumed. We need the real ones.
  • Is there a "what to do now" page behind "Act now: see why this is a priority"? The wording promises the reason for the priority, not actions. If the disease page has actions, we can say so. If not, it stays as it is.
  • Should the Disease cell also carry a short summary? It fits (the card text at 16px) but makes the table about 20% taller. The name cell is the row header, so a screen reader would read the whole summary on each cell of the row. It is not added. The same is true of the More details link in that cell, which research should check.
  • Who writes the reason on each card, and how often? The prototype shows it as written copy. Does anyone also need a summary at the top of the page, what should it say, and does it need to differ by role?
  • How do people reach a measure page? The prototype has no "Look in more detail" line, so people rely on the top navigation. If they miss it, we try column headings as links.
  • Real or fictional disease names in the research? Fictional names avoid distraction, but experts may find it harder to judge whether the results are plausible.
  • Which participants, and how do we recruit people who work by disease and by measure?

Wider questions, such as brand and real data, are in the design log.
