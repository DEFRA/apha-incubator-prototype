Design notes

The decisions we have made, the directions we are taking and what is still open. Update this page when a decision changes.

All data is illustrative and the disease names are fictional. Last updated 10 November 2026.

Where we are

We are designing an early warning service for exotic animal diseases. It brings four kinds of evidence together for each disease: risk of incursion, impact, control measures and response options. Together they give a priority score, so subject matter experts can see which threats need action first.

The team decided not to test the risk page on its own. We will test the priority view first. It is the top-level page, so people meet risk and impact as part of it. We will then use the same sessions to learn what people want from risk, impact, control measures and response options, and build those pages from what they say.

Where each part has got to:

  • Priority view: in design. Three options are prototyped, and we recommend testing option C. See the priority view options.
  • Risk page and disease page: early ideas only, in the wireframes.
  • Impact, control measures and response options pages: not explored yet.

How we got here

Our thinking has changed direction a few times. This is the path, with the documents from each stage.

  1. We set out to test the risk slice. The first brief was to prepare a user test of the risk "slice". We had not defined exactly which screens that meant. See the design brief and the prototype spec.
  2. We tried ideas for risk. We toyed with a few, including which way people should enter risk, and which perspectives to view it from: by disease, by layer, or both. See the wireframes and the structure options.
  3. We saw that we needed to test the wider concept first. Before going deep on risk, we needed to know whether the overall concept works for people. So we changed to concept testing alongside risk. See the earlier test proposal.
  4. We chose the priority view to test the concept. Further discussion suggested that designing and testing the priority view would make the concept more tangible. People can react to something concrete, and we can build outwards from what they understand and tell us. See the priority view test plan.

That is why the priority view comes first, and why the risk, impact, control measures and response options pages will be shaped by what we learn from it.

Decisions made

Decided means the team has agreed it. Recommended means it is our recommendation and the team still needs to confirm it. The test plan has the detail.

Test approach

Decisions about the test approach
Decision Why Status
Test the priority view first, not the risk page on its own Priority is the top level. People meet risk and impact as parts of it, which is closer to how they would use the service Decided
Use the test to find out what people want from risk The priority view is a conversation starter. About a third of each session is discovery: what they would want to know, and what they would decide from it Decided
Use fictional disease names and illustrative data So people do not read made-up numbers as real. The page and the moderator both say so Decided
Test option C with everyone, starting half on the table and half on cards Option C holds both A and B, so every person sees both. Six people split across three pages is too few. The cost is that C cannot isolate A against B Recommended

Content and scoring

Decisions about content and scoring
Decision Why Status
Show the summary at the top, before the table or cards, in every option People need the position quickly, and can go deeper if they choose Decided
Do not repeat the source at the top of the page It is in "Where this information comes from" at the bottom. The test checks whether people find it Decided
Show impact as four areas: Public health, Animal welfare, Wider economy and society, International trade These are the areas the team gave. A disease can have more than one, so they show as a short list. Which disease has which area is made up for the prototype Decided
Show change on the priority view for priority only A "What has changed" line in the summary, and an outlined "Score increased" or "Score decreased" tag with the old score under the score. Diseases that have not changed show nothing. Changes in risk, impact, control measures and response options belong on their own pages. The previous scores are worked out for the prototype, not real Decided
Score priority and each of the four measures out of 5 Priority, risk, impact, control measures and response options are each a score out of 5, shown with a bar and the number. Priority is in four bands: Act now, Prioritise, Monitor and Maintain. The earlier formula and its score out of 140 were fictional, so we dropped them to avoid research questions about the model. The test is of the page, not of the model Given

Layout and visual design

Decisions about layout and visual design
Decision Why Status
Keep one page, and use a wider container (1,200px) for the table The first table felt cramped. Priority is the top level, so it should not be a click away, and five rows fit on a wide page. Text stays at two-thirds width. Revisit if there are more than about 8 to 10 diseases Recommended
Use one header row on the table The double header was visually busy and added cognitive load. A sentence above the table explains the threat and preparedness groups. Rules separate priority from risk, and impact from control measures, so the table reads as three groups. The test checks whether people miss the group headings Decided
Split preparedness into two pages in the top navigation The navigation now matches the table: Priority, Risk, Impact, Control measures and Response options. There is no single Preparedness page, so the open question about one page or two is answered for the prototype. The pages themselves are not designed yet Decided
Give the sorted column a light tint A subtle way to show which column the list is sorted by. The arrow and the screen reader state say the same thing, so colour is not the only cue Decided
Links in the table do not change colour once visited Visited colours across a table of links are a distraction. Hover and focus still change Decided
Drop the matrix with diseases across the top It is not how people think about the problem, and it pushed the summary down the page Decided
Use a white page background, with grey panels An earlier pale green was an approximation of a Defra theme value, not a brand-approved colour Recommended

Navigation and flow

Decisions about navigation and flow
Decision Why Status
Give two routes out of the priority view Some people want more on one disease, and others want to compare one measure across every disease. Disease names link to a disease page. Risk, Impact, Control measures and Response options are in the top navigation and in a "Look in more detail" line Decided
Column headings only sort One control should not do two jobs. If people miss the routes in the test, we try headings as links with a "Sort by" select Recommended
Call the pages Risk, Impact, Control measures and Response options, not "layer" or "slice" "Layer" and "slice" are team words. We watch what people call them Recommended
Let people cross between a disease and a measure, and always get back to Priority From a measure page you can open a disease. From a disease page you can compare every disease on one measure. "Priority" in the top navigation gets you back. These pages are not designed yet Recommended

Accessibility and colour

Decisions about accessibility and colour
Decision Why Status
Colour is never the only cue Every band, risk level, impact level and change has a text label, and rank and score are printed Decided
Use Defra green for borders and accents only White text on it is 3.33:1, which fails the 4.5:1 needed. Link green and the Maintain band use darker greens that pass Decided
Use black text on the Prioritise band White on that orange is 2.78:1 and fails. Black is 7.04:1 Decided
Show preparedness bars in green and threat bars in grey, with the number always printed A high preparedness score is good but a high risk is bad, so red to green would send mixed signals and fails for red-green colour blindness. Bars are a plain fill and the number carries the meaning. The test checks this Decided

Not checked yet: a screen reader pass, 200% zoom and text spacing, and forced colours mode. We need an accessibility review before testing with users.

Directions we are taking

Priority first, then grow outward

Priority is the top level, so we test it first. What people tell us they want from risk, impact, control measures and response options shapes those pages, rather than our assumptions.

One page, two ways to read it

Option C puts a table and a set of cards on one page, with a switch between them. The table suits comparing one measure across diseases. The cards suit "what do I do next?", grouped by action. We will see which people choose, when they switch and why. If one view is rarely used, we drop it.

Two ways in, one way back

People start on Priority, then go to a disease or to a measure across every disease. They can cross between the two, and the top navigation always gets them back to Priority. The user flows show this.

Build on the early risk ideas

The wireframes are very early thinking, not designs. Options 1A to 1C could become a page showing every disease on one measure. Option 2C, priority and four layers, is the closest to a disease page showing all four measures. We will shape these from the test sessions.

Prototype in this repository

When the priority view design is refined, we will rebuild it here as prototype pages using GOV.UK Frontend, so we can test it with users. The pages in this resource are static snapshots until then.

Open questions

  • Brand. The prototype uses a Defra look: system font, green links, and a black header with a green stripe. The brief says GOV.UK only. Which do we put in front of users?
  • Who writes the summary and the one-line reasons on the cards, and how often? The prototype shows them as written copy.
  • "Preparedness" or "vulnerability"? The scoring framework uses "vulnerability". The prototype says "Preparedness". Do we test both?
  • Do we show "No change" on every row? Showing only changes keeps the table calmer, but people may not tell "no change" from "not assessed".
  • Real or fictional disease names in the test? Fictional names avoid distraction, but experts may find it harder to judge whether the results are plausible.
  • Which participants, and how do we recruit people who work by disease and by measure?
  • How do people reach a measure page? If they miss both the navigation and the "Look in more detail" line, we try column headings as links.
  • Sections or pages? Is each of risk, impact, control measures and response options a separate page or a section of one page? Does impact need its own page?
  • Scales. The prototype shows every score out of 5. How do the five risk levels in the monthly report map to a score out of 5? Does impact change over time, or is it fixed until reassessed?
  • Real data. Which diseases have which impact areas, and what were last month’s scores? Both are made up in the prototype.

What happens next

  1. Agree the options and the open questions above as a team.
  2. Do an accessibility review before any testing with users.
  3. Recruit participants and pilot the session with one person.
  4. Run the sessions, then group what we learn into issues and wants.
  5. Use what we learn to shape the Risk, Impact, Control measures and Response options pages.
  6. Rebuild the refined design as prototype pages in this repository.
