Where we are

We are designing an early warning service for exotic animal diseases. It brings four kinds of evidence together for each disease: risk of incursion, impact, control measures and response options. Together they give a priority score, so subject matter experts can see which threats need action first.

The team decided not to test the risk page on its own. We will test the priority view first. It is the top-level page, so people meet risk and impact as part of it. We will then use the same sessions to learn what people want from risk, impact, control measures and response options, and build those pages from what they say.

Where each part has got to:

  • Priority view: in design. Three options are prototyped, and we recommend testing option C. Option C has been developed further than A and B, which still show the earlier design and will be brought into line later. See the priority view options.
  • Risk page and disease page: early ideas only, in the wireframes.
  • Impact, control measures and response options pages: not explored yet.

Decision pages for option C. Each compares the choices we looked at and records which one we chose:

  • Arranging the priority cell
  • Showing control measures and response options as checklists
  • Bars under the risk and impact levels
  • Explaining the measures on the cards
  • Showing when a priority score changed

How we got here

Our thinking has changed direction a few times. This is the path, with the documents from each stage.

  1. We set out to test the risk slice. The first brief was to prepare a user test of the risk "slice". We had not defined exactly which screens that meant.
  2. We tried ideas for risk. We toyed with a few, including which way people should enter risk, and which perspectives to view it from: by disease, by layer, or both. See the wireframes and the structure options.
  3. We saw that we needed to test the wider concept first. Before going deep on risk, we needed to know whether the overall concept works for people. So we changed to concept testing alongside risk.
  4. We chose the priority view to test the concept. Further discussion suggested that designing and testing the priority view would make the concept more tangible. People can react to something concrete, and we can build outwards from what they understand and tell us. See the notes for the user researcher.

That is why the priority view comes first, and why the risk, impact, control measures and response options pages will be shaped by what we learn from it.

How we went from 12 needs to 9

The user needs we agreed on 26 October 2026 were twelve, in three themes. On 9 November we combined three pairs that overlapped, giving nine needs in the same three themes. The notes for the researcher and the early ideas now use the nine. The evidence behind each need is held by the user research team and is not published here.

  • UN1 One trusted source combines the earlier "One trusted source" and "One shared source". Both described one place everyone trusts that tells them when something changes.
  • UN2 Early warning signal combines "When risk changes" and "Agreed trigger point". The signal is in scope. Agreeing what happens when it changes, and who acts, is organisational, so that part sits with governance.
  • UN8 Right level of detail combines "Short operational summary" and "Right level of detail". Both were about getting information at the depth each role needs.

The other six carry across unchanged and are renumbered.

Decisions made

Content and scoring

Decisions about content and scoring
Decision Why Status
Do not show a summary at the top of the page. Keep a one-line reason on each card We first had a plain-language summary above the list (with a "What has changed" line) as well as a reason on each card. We removed the top summary on 11 November. It had to cover every section at once, so it was too long, and one summary can't be short and right for everyone, because people in different roles want different things from it. The reason on each card stays. It is about one disease, it sits next to the scores it explains, and it is short enough to read in place. The test asks whether anyone also wants a summary at the top, and what it would say for their role Decided
Do not explain the scores in a paragraph above the list The paragraph under the list heading repeated the priority actions key and the details panel on how the score is worked out. We removed it on 11 November, with the visible heading, so the list starts straight after the view switch. The heading is still there for screen readers. The key at the bottom, the level names beside risk and impact, and the details panel now carry the explanation. One thing to watch: the paragraph also said how to read each measure. The key at the bottom covers that now, and research could check whether people still get it Decided
Do not repeat the source at the top of the page It is in "Where this information comes from" at the bottom. The test checks whether people find it Decided
Show impact as four areas: Public health, Animal welfare, Wider economy and society, International trade These are the areas the team gave. A disease can have more than one, so they show as a short list. Which disease has which area is made up for the prototype Decided
Show change on the priority view for priority only, with a date on every row In the table, a changed score shows "Up from 12" or "Down from 16" with a small arrow at the end of that line. On the cards it shows "Previous score 16" and an outlined "Score increased" or "Score decreased" tag underneath, because cards have more room and the arrow looked odd there. Every row also says "Changed [date]", so a score that has been stable for months can be told from one nobody has checked. We chose a date under every score over a separate sortable column and over a compare-over-a-period control. The earlier "no arrows" decision is reversed for the table, and the words Up and Down are always written, so shape and colour are never the only cue. Changes in risk, impact, control measures and response options belong on their own pages. The previous scores and dates are made up for the prototype. See showing when a priority score changed Decided
Priority is risk × impact, out of 25, in four priority actions Risk and impact are each rated 1 to 5. Act now is 15 or more, Prioritise is 10 to 14, Monitor is 4 to 9 and Maintain is 1 to 3. We call them priority actions, not bands, because "band" is a word we made up. Tags are sentence case, not capitals, because capitals are harder to read. Control measures and response options do not change the score. The earlier out of 5 scores and the score out of 140 were fictional and are gone. This is the prototype’s model, not the real one, and the real link from preparedness to priority is an open question Decided
Show risk and impact as group names, not numbers Risk is Rare, Unlikely, Moderate, Likely or Almost certain. Impact is Insignificant, Minor, Significant, Major or Severe. These are the words people already use with the risk matrix. The 1 to 5 number is still behind them. A key at the bottom of the page lists all five levels. The test checks that "Minor" reads as the second of five levels and not as a minor concern Decided
Show control measures and response options as checklists, not scores Each shows one line, "7 of 8 need review", and a segmented bar with red segments for the items still to review. There is no colour block and no 1 to 5 score. We chose this over a score, a count with a colour block, and a plain count. We assumed 10 control items and 8 response items, and the real totals are not known. See checklist options Decided
Take the grey bars off the risk and impact levels With a level name on a colour block, the bars said the same thing twice. We removed them to test whether anyone misses them. See bars under risk and impact Recommended
Show a "Confirmed in the UK" state for a disease that has arrived A disease already here is a different state, not a higher level of risk. It has a purple tag (8.7:1 contrast), which sits outside the green to red scale, with the date and number of premises. Scaly claw fever is the example Decided
Remove the rank. Sort by priority score, highest first A rank number gave false precision and made a tie look like a difference. The order is still by score, and nothing is numbered Decided
Explain the four measures in a key at the bottom, not as hints on the cards Hints under every label made the cards busy. We tried a hover tooltip (fails WCAG 1.4.13), a toggletip, a details panel on each card and one panel at the top. All were repetitive or sat too far from the cards. The key "What the four measures mean" lists all five levels of risk and impact, what control measures and response options mean, and how to read the red bars. See card hint options Decided
Set the priority cell as plain lines under the tag The cell reads "Score 12", "Down from 16" and "Changed 5 November 2026" as plain lines in one style, under the tag. The date is at the bottom of the cell in smaller text (16px) so each row ends in the same place. See priority cell options Decided

Layout and visual design

Keep one page, and use a wider container (1,200px) for the table The first table felt cramped. Priority is the top level, so it should not be a click away, and five rows fit on a wide page. Text stays at two-thirds width. Revisit if there are more than about 8 to 10 diseases Recommended
Use one header row on the table The double header was visually busy and added cognitive load. A sentence above the table explains the threat and preparedness groups. Rules separate priority from risk, and impact from control measures, so the table reads as three groups. The test checks whether people miss the group headings Decided
Split preparedness into two pages in the top navigation The navigation now matches the table: Priority, Risk, Impact, Control measures and Response options. There is no single Preparedness page, so the open question about one page or two is answered for the prototype. The pages themselves are not designed yet Decided
Give the sorted column a light tint A subtle way to show which column the list is sorted by. The arrow and the screen reader state say the same thing, so colour is not the only cue Decided
Links in the table do not change colour once visited Visited colours across a table of links are a distraction. Hover and focus still change Decided
Drop the matrix with diseases across the top It is not how people think about the problem, and it pushed the content down the page Decided
Use a white page background, with grey panels An earlier pale green was an approximation of a Defra theme value, not a brand-approved colour Recommended
Show the cards two across, with more space, and a More details link after each measure The cards felt busy. At 64em and wider they sit two across, with more padding, bold dark labels and no hints. A "More details" link follows each of the four measures, lined up across cards, and its hidden text names the measure and the disease. Cards that were full width looked too much like the table rows Decided
Disease names are plain titles. A More details link goes to the disease page The link sits under the name in the table, and after the summary on each card. For the two Act now diseases it reads "Act now: see why this is a priority", to encourage action without promising content we may not have. "See what to do" and "View disease details" were rejected: we are not sure a "what to do now" page exists, and "disease details" reads as facts about the disease. The test checks what people expect behind each link, and whether they click the plain-text name Recommended
Keep the Disease column wide enough for the longest name A wrapped disease name was hard to read in one of the most important columns. The column now has a minimum width, the sortable headings can wrap, and the page container stays at 1,200px. The cost is that the Risk, Control measures and Response options columns are narrower, and "need review" wraps to a second line. We looked at abbreviating months in the date line to give some width back Decided
Remove the "Look in more detail" section from option C The More details links and the top navigation now do the same job. Options A and B still have it Decided

Accessibility and colour

Decisions about accessibility and colour
Decision Why Status
Colour is never the only cue Every priority action, risk level, impact level and change has a text label, and the score is printed. The checklist bar always sits under its "N of M need review" count, and the arrow is always paired with the words Up or Down Decided
Use Defra green for borders and accents only White text on it is 3.33:1, which fails the 4.5:1 needed. Link green and the Maintain band use darker greens that pass Decided
Use black text on the Prioritise band White on that orange is 2.78:1 and fails. Black is 7.04:1 Decided
Lead with a muted colour block in the table for risk and impact: the level name, with the number hidden The blocks use the same scale for both measures (green for the lowest level, through yellow, to red for the highest). They are paler than the priority actions so priority stays the loudest thing on the page. Contrast is 5.7 to 7.8:1. Control measures and response options do not use the scale at all, because a high count of items needing review is bad and a high risk is also bad, so the two no longer pull in different directions Decided
Show the items needing review in dark red segments, with the count always printed Red segments (#942514) on white with a grey outline for the segments. Red against green is only 1.55:1, so a two-colour red and green bar was rejected, because it fails for colour blindness. The count carries the meaning and the bar is decoration (hidden from screen readers) Decided
Use sentence case for the priority tags Capitals are harder to read, and GOV.UK content guidance says not to use them. The tags read Act now, Prioritise, Monitor and Maintain. This is done on option C only for now. Screen readers read the text the same either way, and contrast is unchanged Decided
Keep card labels as plain text, and name each More details link Sending people away before they have read the card is not accessible. Every More details link has hidden text saying which measure and disease it is for. Text on the cards is 16px or more on desktop. The GOV.UK small text drops to 14px below about 640px wide, which the notes for the user researcher flag Decided

Not checked yet: a screen reader pass, 200% zoom and text spacing, and forced colours mode. We need an accessibility review before testing with users.

Notes for the user researcher

These notes are from the design team, to help you plan the research. They are not a test plan. The method, tasks, participants, session structure and analysis are for you and the wider team to decide.

What is ready to test

Option C of the priority view: one page with a switch between a table and cards. Options A and B have not had the design changes made to C, so they are not planned for testing. All data is illustrative and the disease names are fictional.

Which view people see first

This is for you and the wider team to decide: cards first, table first, or alternating between them. Some things that may help:

  • Cards lead with a short reason for each disease and group the diseases by priority action. The table suits comparing one measure across diseases, and can be sorted.
  • The view people see first may shape what they do next. Alternating spreads that across participants, but needs enough people on each starting view.
  • Either view opens directly from a link, and the choice is kept in the address, so a link shares the view.

What we would like to learn

These are the design team’s questions. Use, change or replace them.

  • Can people tell which diseases need action first, and why?
  • Do they read the level names, the "need review" checklists and the "Changed" date the way we intend?
  • What would they want from risk, impact, control measures and response options pages? The priority view is a starting point for that conversation.
  • Which view do they use, do they switch, and why?
  • What do they expect to find behind each "More details" link?

What is made up

  • The scores, change dates and previous scores, and which disease has which impact areas.
  • The model: priority is risk × impact out of 25, with cut-offs at 15, 10 and 4. Control measures and response options do not change the score.
  • The checklist totals (10 control items and 8 response items), and how often risk and impact are updated.

Before testing with users

Colour contrast was checked as we designed, and every pair passes 4.5:1 for text. An accessibility review is still needed, and the automated contrast check should be run again on the latest version. See the open questions and the decisions made on option C for what is still undecided.

Directions we are taking

Priority first, then grow outward

Priority is the top level, so we test it first. What people tell us they want from risk, impact, control measures and response options shapes those pages, rather than our assumptions.

One page, two ways to read it

Option C puts a table and a set of cards on one page, with a switch between them. The table suits comparing one measure across diseases. The cards suit "what do I do next?", grouped by action. We will see which people choose, when they switch and why. If one view is rarely used, we drop it.

Two ways in, one way back

People start on Priority, then go to a disease or to a measure across every disease. They can cross between the two, and the top navigation always gets them back to Priority. The user flows show this.

Build on the early risk ideas

The wireframes are very early thinking, not designs. Options 1A to 1C could become a page showing every disease on one measure. Option 2C, priority and four layers, is the closest to a disease page showing all four measures. We will shape these from the test sessions.

Prototype in this repository

When the priority view design is refined, we will rebuild it here as prototype pages using GOV.UK Frontend, so we can test it with users. The pages in this resource are static snapshots until then.

Open questions

  • How does preparedness link into priority? The new formula is risk × impact only (11 November). Control measures and response options are checklists of items needing review, and they sit beside the priority score without changing it. So a well-prepared disease and a badly prepared one with the same risk and impact get the same priority. Is that what we want? Should the checklists lower or raise the score, set the band, or only inform what to do (the "Act now" and "Prioritise" advice)? This needs an answer from the model owners before we show how priority is worked out.
  • Brand. The prototype uses a Defra look: system font, green links, and a black header with a green stripe. The brief says GOV.UK only. Which do we put in front of users?
  • "Preparedness" or "vulnerability"? The scoring framework uses "vulnerability". The prototype says "Preparedness". Do we test both?
  • Who writes the reason on each card, and how often? The prototype shows it as written copy. Does anyone also need a summary at the top of the page, what should it say, and does it need to differ by role?
  • What does "Changed [date]" mean? The prototype shows the date a priority score last changed, on every row. Does it mean the score value changed, or that risk and impact were last checked, even if the score stayed the same? The second is probably better for trust, but harder to explain. Do we need a rule for how long a change tag stays, and do we have the dates and previous scores for every disease?
  • Real or fictional disease names in the test? Fictional names avoid distraction, but experts may find it harder to judge whether the results are plausible.
  • Which participants, and how do we recruit people who work by disease and by measure?
  • How do people reach a measure page? Option C has no "Look in more detail" line now, so they rely on the top navigation. If they miss it, we try column headings as links.
  • Sections or pages? Is each of risk, impact, control measures and response options a separate page or a section of one page? Does impact need its own page?
  • Levels and checklists. The prototype shows risk and impact as five named levels, which should line up with the five risk levels in the monthly report. Do they? Does impact change over time, or is it fixed until reassessed? The checklist totals (10 control items and 8 response items), how often each is updated and the priority cut-offs (15, 10 and 4) are all assumed. We need the real ones.
  • Is there a "what to do now" page behind "Act now: see why this is a priority"? The wording promises the reason for the priority, not actions. If the disease page has actions, we can say so. If not, it stays as it is.
  • Should the Disease cell also carry a short summary? It fits (the card text at 16px) but makes the table about 20% taller. The name cell is the row header, so a screen reader would read the whole summary on each cell of the row. It is not added. The same is true of the More details link in that cell, which the test should check.
  • Real data. Which diseases have which impact areas, and what were the previous scores and change dates? Both are made up in the prototype.
  • Internal source names. The prototype names internal data sources (IDM, D2R2, Risk ID and the preparedness indices) in "Where this information comes from". This is a public repository. Is that fine, or should we generalise them before the next push?

What happens next

  1. Agree the open questions above as a team.
  2. Do an accessibility review before any testing with users.
  3. The user researcher plans the sessions, using the notes above as a starting point.
  4. Run the sessions, then group what we learn into issues and wants.
  5. Use what we learn to shape the Risk, Impact, Control measures and Response options pages.
  6. Rebuild the refined design as prototype pages in this repository.
