Priority view design decisions

The decisions behind priority view prototype v1.1 and the earlier v1.0, and the directions we are taking. Update this page when a decision changes.

All data is illustrative and the disease names are fictional. Last updated 12 November 2026. The journey so far and what we have learned are in the design log. Notes for the user researcher and the open questions are on the priority view design test page.

On this page

  • Where we are
  • Decisions made
  • Directions we are taking
  • Open questions and what happens next

Where we are

We are designing an early warning service for animal diseases, which are currently fictional. It brings four kinds of evidence together for each disease: risk of incursion, impact, control measures and response options. Together they give a priority score, so subject matter experts can see which threats need action first.

The team decided not to test the risk page on its own. Round 1 of usability testing starts with the priority view. It is the top-level page, so people meet risk and impact as part of it. Round 1 also explores what people want from risk, impact, control measures and response options, so we can build those pages from what they say.

Where each part has got to:

  • Priority view: prototype v1.1 is ready for round 1, in two versions. The test leads with combined preparedness, and separate preparedness measures are there to show if combined is flagged as not working. It is option C, a table and cards on one page. Options A and B are earlier designs, not updated with the later changes and not planned for testing. See the priority view design test and the priority view options.
  • Risk page and disease page: early ideas only, in the wireframes. See risk exploration.
  • Impact, control measures and response options pages: not explored yet.

Decision pages for option C. Each compares the choices we looked at and records which one we chose:

  • Arranging the priority cell
  • Showing control measures and response options as checklists
  • Bars under the risk and impact levels
  • Explaining the measures on the cards
  • Showing when a priority score changed

How our thinking changed direction and what we have learned are in the design log.

Decisions made

Decided means the team has agreed it. Recommended means it is our recommendation and the team still needs to confirm it. To decide means it is for the user researcher and the wider team.

After the team design review (v1.1)

These decisions came from the team design review on 12 November 2026 and are in prototype v1.1, in both preparedness versions. Where they replace an earlier decision below, the earlier one says so. More updates from the review will be added. The design log records the review.

Decisions after the team design review
Decision Why Status
Lead the test with combined preparedness. Show the separate version if combined is flagged as not working Combined preparedness is the better starting point. The separate version is ready as the alternative, and the combined page is the main entry point. How people start (cards or table, or alternating) is for the user researcher and the team Decided
Remove Scaly claw fever and the "Confirmed in the UK" state The service will not cover diseases that are already active in the UK. Five fictional diseases remain Decided
Show preparedness two ways: control measures and response options separately, and combined as one measure We do not know whether people need prevention and response shown separately. Combined preparedness uses five statuses instead of a count of items: Totally prepared, Largely prepared, Moderately prepared, Slightly prepared and Not at all prepared. Red means the worse end, as for risk and impact. Which version works better is for research To test
The priority score combines all four measures The priority scoring team member said priority should combine all four columns. The prototype uses a fictional process: threat (risk of incursion 1 to 6 × impact 1 to 5, up to 30) multiplied by 1 plus a preparedness gap (the average share of control measure and response option items that need review). That gives a score out of 60 with priority actions at 30, 20 and 8. In the combined version, the gap sets the preparedness status. The real scoring will be worked out later. The design only needs to show all four have been considered Decided
Risk of incursion has six levels: Very high, High, Medium, Low, Very low and Negligible These replace Rare to Almost certain. Each level has its own muted colour, so Medium is orange. Contrast is 5.7 to 7.8:1 Decided
Remove the impact areas (Animal welfare, International trade, Wider economy and society) It was not clear what they added at this level Decided
Remove the change display, and ask users what change they want surfaced The "Changed" date, previous score, arrows and increased and decreased tags rested on too many assumptions. Research will ask what change people want to see, why, and what they would do with it, then we design from there Decided

Test approach

Decisions about the test approach
Decision Why Status
Test the priority view first, not the risk page on its own Priority is the top level. People meet risk and impact as parts of it, which is closer to how they would use the service Decided
Use the test to find out what people want from risk The priority view is a conversation starter, so it can also help us learn what people would want to know, and what they would decide from it. How much of a session that takes is for the user researcher to decide Decided
Use fictional disease names and illustrative data So people do not read made-up numbers as real. The page and the moderator both say so Decided
Test option C only. Options A and B are not planned for testing Option C holds both the table of A and the cards of B, so one page lets us compare table and cards. Options A and B have not had the design changes made to C, so they would not be a fair test. We would bring them into line first if a like-for-like comparison is wanted later Decided
Which view people see first: cards, table, or alternating between them Cards first suits a "what do I act on?" start. Alternating spreads the effect of what people see first across participants. This is for the user researcher and the wider team to decide. See the notes for the user researcher To decide

Content and scoring

Decisions about content and scoring
Decision Why Status
Do not show a summary at the top of the page. Keep a one-line reason on each card We first had a plain-language summary above the list (with a "What has changed" line) as well as a reason on each card. We removed the top summary on 11 November. It had to cover every section at once, so it was too long, and one summary can't be short and right for everyone, because people in different roles want different things from it. The reason on each card stays. It is about one disease, it sits next to the scores it explains, and it is short enough to read in place. The test asks whether anyone also wants a summary at the top, and what it would say for their role Decided
Do not explain the scores in a paragraph above the list The paragraph under the list heading repeated the priority actions key and the details panel on how the score is worked out. We removed it on 11 November, with the visible heading, so the list starts straight after the view switch. The heading is still there for screen readers. The key at the bottom, the level names beside risk and impact, and the details panel now carry the explanation. One thing to watch: the paragraph also said how to read each measure. The key at the bottom covers that now, and research could check whether people still get it Decided
Do not repeat the source at the top of the page It is in "Where this information comes from" at the bottom. The test checks whether people find it Decided
Show impact as four areas: Public health, Animal welfare, Wider economy and society, International trade These are the areas the team gave. A disease can have more than one, so they show as a short list. Which disease has which area is made up for the prototype Decided
Superseded in v1.1: Show change on the priority view for priority only, with a date on every row In the table, a changed score shows "Up from 12" or "Down from 16" with a small arrow at the end of that line. On the cards it shows "Previous score 16" and an outlined "Score increased" or "Score decreased" tag underneath, because cards have more room and the arrow looked odd there. Every row also says "Changed [date]", so a score that has been stable for months can be told from one nobody has checked. We chose a date under every score over a separate sortable column and over a compare-over-a-period control. The earlier "no arrows" decision is reversed for the table, and the words Up and Down are always written, so shape and colour are never the only cue. Changes in risk, impact, control measures and response options belong on their own pages. The previous scores and dates are made up for the prototype. See showing when a priority score changed Decided
Superseded in v1.1: Priority is risk × impact, out of 25, in four priority actions Risk and impact are each rated 1 to 5. Act now is 15 or more, Prioritise is 10 to 14, Monitor is 4 to 9 and Maintain is 1 to 3. We call them priority actions, not bands, because "band" is a word we made up. Tags are sentence case, not capitals, because capitals are harder to read. Control measures and response options do not change the score. The earlier out of 5 scores and the score out of 140 were fictional and are gone. This is the prototype’s model, not the real one, and the real link from preparedness to priority is an open question Decided
Partly superseded in v1.1 (risk now has six levels): Show risk and impact as group names, not numbers Risk is Rare, Unlikely, Moderate, Likely or Almost certain. Impact is Insignificant, Minor, Significant, Major or Severe. These are the words people already use with the risk matrix. The 1 to 5 number is still behind them. A key at the bottom of the page lists all five levels. The test checks that "Minor" reads as the second of five levels and not as a minor concern Decided
Show control measures and response options as checklists, not scores Each shows one line, "7 of 8 need review", and a segmented bar with red segments for the items still to review. There is no colour block and no 1 to 5 score. We chose this over a score, a count with a colour block, and a plain count. We assumed 10 control items and 8 response items, and the real totals are not known. See checklist options Decided
Take the grey bars off the risk and impact levels With a level name on a colour block, the bars said the same thing twice. We removed them to test whether anyone misses them. See bars under risk and impact Recommended
Superseded in v1.1: Show a "Confirmed in the UK" state for a disease that has arrived A disease already here is a different state, not a higher level of risk. It has a purple tag (8.7:1 contrast), which sits outside the green to red scale, with the date and number of premises. Scaly claw fever is the example Decided
Remove the rank. Sort by priority score, highest first A rank number gave false precision and made a tie look like a difference. The order is still by score, and nothing is numbered Decided
Explain the four measures in a key at the bottom, not as hints on the cards Hints under every label made the cards busy. We tried a hover tooltip (fails WCAG 1.4.13), a toggletip, a details panel on each card and one panel at the top. All were repetitive or sat too far from the cards. The key "What the four measures mean" lists all five levels of risk and impact, what control measures and response options mean, and how to read the red bars. See card hint options Decided
Partly superseded in v1.1 (no change lines): Set the priority cell as plain lines under the tag The cell reads "Score 12", "Down from 16" and "Changed 5 November 2026" as plain lines in one style, under the tag. The date is at the bottom of the cell in smaller text (16px) so each row ends in the same place. See priority cell options Decided

Layout and visual design

Decisions about layout and visual design
Decision Why Status
Keep one page, and use a wider container (1,200px) for the table The first table felt cramped. Priority is the top level, so it should not be a click away, and five rows fit on a wide page. Text stays at two-thirds width. Revisit if there are more than about 8 to 10 diseases Recommended
Use one header row on the table The double header was visually busy and added cognitive load. A sentence above the table explains the threat and preparedness groups. Rules separate priority from risk, and impact from control measures, so the table reads as three groups. The test checks whether people miss the group headings Decided
Split preparedness into two pages in the top navigation The navigation now matches the table: Priority, Risk, Impact, Control measures and Response options. There is no single Preparedness page, so the open question about one page or two is answered for the prototype. The pages themselves are not designed yet Decided
Give the sorted column a light tint A subtle way to show which column the list is sorted by. The arrow and the screen reader state say the same thing, so colour is not the only cue Decided
Links in the table do not change colour once visited Visited colours across a table of links are a distraction. Hover and focus still change Decided
Drop the matrix with diseases across the top It is not how people think about the problem, and it pushed the content down the page Decided
Use a white page background, with grey panels An earlier pale green was an approximation of a Defra theme value, not a brand-approved colour Recommended
Show the cards two across, with more space, and a More details link after each measure The cards felt busy. At 64em and wider they sit two across, with more padding, bold dark labels and no hints. A "More details" link follows each of the four measures, lined up across cards, and its hidden text names the measure and the disease. Cards that were full width looked too much like the table rows Decided
Disease names are plain titles. A More details link goes to the disease page The link sits under the name in the table, and after the summary on each card. For the two Act now diseases it reads "Act now: see why this is a priority", to encourage action without promising content we may not have. "See what to do" and "View disease details" were rejected: we are not sure a "what to do now" page exists, and "disease details" reads as facts about the disease. The test checks what people expect behind each link, and whether they click the plain-text name Recommended
Keep the Disease column wide enough for the longest name A wrapped disease name was hard to read in one of the most important columns. The column now has a minimum width, the sortable headings can wrap, and the page container stays at 1,200px. The cost is that the Risk, Control measures and Response options columns are narrower, and "need review" wraps to a second line. We looked at abbreviating months in the date line to give some width back Decided
Remove the "Look in more detail" section from option C The More details links and the top navigation now do the same job. Options A and B still have it Decided

Navigation and flow

Decisions about navigation and flow
Decision Why Status
Give two routes out of the priority view Some people want more on one disease, and others want to compare one measure across every disease. Disease names are plain titles, with a "More details" link to a disease page. Risk, Impact, Control measures and Response options are in the top navigation. Options A and B still have a "Look in more detail" line as well Decided
Column headings only sort One control should not do two jobs. If people miss the routes in the test, we try headings as links with a "Sort by" select Recommended
Call the pages Risk, Impact, Control measures and Response options, not "layer" or "slice" "Layer" and "slice" are team words. We watch what people call them Recommended
Let people cross between a disease and a measure, and always get back to Priority From a measure page you can open a disease. From a disease page you can compare every disease on one measure. "Priority" in the top navigation gets you back. These pages are not designed yet Recommended

Accessibility and colour

Decisions about accessibility and colour
Decision Why Status
Colour is never the only cue Every priority action, risk level, impact level and change has a text label, and the score is printed. The checklist bar always sits under its "N of M need review" count, and the arrow is always paired with the words Up or Down Decided
Use Defra green for borders and accents only White text on it is 3.33:1, which fails the 4.5:1 needed. Link green and the Maintain band use darker greens that pass Decided
Use black text on the Prioritise band White on that orange is 2.78:1 and fails. Black is 7.04:1 Decided
Lead with a muted colour block in the table for risk and impact: the level name, with the number hidden The blocks use the same scale for both measures (green for the lowest level, through yellow, to red for the highest). They are paler than the priority actions so priority stays the loudest thing on the page. Contrast is 5.7 to 7.8:1. Control measures and response options do not use the scale at all, because a high count of items needing review is bad and a high risk is also bad, so the two no longer pull in different directions Decided
Show the items needing review in dark red segments, with the count always printed Red segments (#942514) on white with a grey outline for the segments. Red against green is only 1.55:1, so a two-colour red and green bar was rejected, because it fails for colour blindness. The count carries the meaning and the bar is decoration (hidden from screen readers) Decided
Use sentence case for the priority tags Capitals are harder to read, and GOV.UK content guidance says not to use them. The tags read Act now, Prioritise, Monitor and Maintain. This is done on option C only for now. Screen readers read the text the same either way, and contrast is unchanged Decided
Keep card labels as plain text, and name each More details link Sending people away before they have read the card is not accessible. Every More details link has hidden text saying which measure and disease it is for. Text on the cards is 16px or more on desktop. The GOV.UK small text drops to 14px below about 640px wide, which the notes for the user researcher flag Decided

Not checked yet: a screen reader pass, 200% zoom and text spacing, and forced colours mode. We need an accessibility review before testing with users.

Notes for the user researcher, including what is not yet checked for accessibility, are on the priority view design test page.

Directions we are taking

Priority first, then grow outward

Priority is the top level, so we test it first. What people tell us they want from risk, impact, control measures and response options shapes those pages, rather than our assumptions.

One page, two ways to read it

Option C puts a table and a set of cards on one page, with a switch between them. The table suits comparing one measure across diseases. The cards suit "what do I do next?", grouped by action. We will see which people choose, when they switch and why. If one view is rarely used, we drop it.

Two ways in, one way back

People start on Priority, then go to a disease or to a measure across every disease. They can cross between the two, and the top navigation always gets them back to Priority. The user flows show this.

Build on the early risk ideas

The wireframes are very early thinking, not designs. Options 1A to 1C could become a page showing every disease on one measure. Option 2C, priority and four layers, is the closest to a disease page showing all four measures. We will shape these from the test sessions.

Prototype in this repository

When the priority view design is refined, we will rebuild it here as prototype pages using GOV.UK Frontend, so we can test it with users. The pages in this resource are static snapshots until then.

Open questions and what happens next

The open questions are with the part they affect: the priority view design test, the risk exploration and the design log for wider ones.

  1. Agree the open questions as a team.
  2. Do an accessibility review before any testing with users.
  3. The user researcher plans the sessions, using the round 1 notes as a starting point.
  4. Run the sessions, then group what we learn into issues and wants, and add them to the design log.
  5. Use what we learn to shape the Risk, Impact, Control measures and Response options pages.
  6. Rebuild the refined design as prototype pages in this repository.
