For user research

Open questions for research

Questions the round 2 design choices have raised that research needs to answer. This is the first pass and it will grow.

What to put in front of participants is on things to test. Questions for the team, not for participants, are on questions for the team.

Reading preparedness

  1. Do people read “x of 10 actions complete” and “y actions still to do” the right way round? In round 1 most people read the earlier wording the opposite way. We don’t yet know whether the new wording fixes the misreading or just changes it.
  2. Does “not at all prepared” ever apply in practice? Does “totally prepared”? One person said there is always some preparing, even by chance.
  3. Which reads more clearly, a count or a percentage? Does either lead people to assume the actions are equal in effort or importance? (They are not weighted, and they vary in complexity.)
  4. Is “action ready” understood as “actions in place”, or as “ready to take action”? The wording suggested in the meeting is ambiguous.
  5. Do people want to see what the ten actions are, and where would they expect to find them?
  6. Is it helpful to see the two parts of preparedness on the front page at all? The names “Stopping it arriving” and “Responding if it arrives” are placeholders.

Reading the page

  1. Can people pick out the highest priority disease, and say why?
  2. Do the “i” buttons get used, and does seeing the levels in order help? The key at the foot of the page was missed in round 1 and is still there, so we can compare.
  3. Do people understand what the priority score means from its “i”? The one-sentence explanation was written by the design team and is not checked against the real model.
  4. Do people expect the column headings to be links, and where do they expect them to go?

Detail pages and expectations

  1. Can people say why the African swine fever risk rating is very high, using the risk page?
  2. What do people expect to see under Impact and Preparedness for a disease?
  3. What do people expect on a page for risk, impact or preparedness across all diseases, and do they need one at all?
  4. Does the risk page answer the questions people have, using only the confirmed topics? What is missing, inside a card or as a whole card?
  5. Is the disease page summary enough, or do people click through to the risk page? Does the summary repeat too much of it?

Change

  1. Can people tell what changed on African swine fever, and when and why? Do they find the “Changed” tag and the “Changes to this rating” table without help?
  2. Which changes would people want to know about, and how? Waiting on Tess’s follow-up email to participants. This is a separate question from whether people can read a change, so keep the two apart in the session.
  3. Which measures do people want changes shown for: risk only, or impact and preparedness too? Risk moves most, impact rarely, and preparedness may change so often it overwhelms.
  4. What makes a change trusted: who made the call, what it is based on, when it was reviewed, and whether it will last?
  5. Is a history of changes really needed, and for how long: the last change, the last 12 months, or since the rating began? Do people open it unprompted? The example table has three invented changes, so check it doesn’t lead people to expect that many.
  6. Do people ask how big a preparedness gap is and how long it would take to close, without being prompted? Raised in round 1 but held back from the design, so listen for it.

Views and layout

  1. Does the assigned view (table or cards) change how well people complete the same task?
  2. Is the table better for the overall picture and the cards better once more detail is added?
  3. Do people need to sort or filter, and if so, by what?

Naming and accessibility

  1. How would people describe the product to a colleague? What would they call it? “Dashboard” may set the wrong expectations, and “tool” may suggest more than the service does. Ask at the end, before showing any names.
  2. Do screen reader users understand the linked headings, the “i” buttons and the hidden table summary? Are “By risk” in the navigation and the “Risk of incursion” heading link confused? Nothing has been tested with a real screen reader yet.

Notes for the researcher

  • The ratings are invented. If anyone questions them, say so. A realistic spread of statuses may teach us more than matching round 1, so don’t worry if people try to correct the figures.
  • On the percentage version, ask what they think it measures, and whether they expect some actions to count for more than others (weighting).
  • The impact, preparedness and cross-disease pages are plain “What would you expect to see here?” prompts. Prompt with the topic list below only if someone is stuck.
  • The African swine fever pages are the only ones with content. Everything on them, including the assessing group, is invented.

What people expected in round 1

  • The disease: species, whether it spreads to people, how it spreads, current policy or guidance, when it was last reviewed.
  • Risk: what each level means with a time frame, source and evidence, whether a change is fast and lasting, how close the outbreak is.
  • Impact: human health, trade and economy (with a scale), effect on operations and staff, which model or report it came from.
  • Preparedness: which areas fall short and by how much, what each counted item is, how old each item is, a link to the preparedness indices, and capacity such as “have I got enough people”.
