Priority view test plan

Status: Draft for team review · Prototype: option A Supersedes the "risk slice on its own" test in earlier test proposal. Sources: design brief, prototype spec and the journeys and mapping board (5 Nov 2026).

Why this test, and why first

The team has decided not to test the risk slice on its own. Priority is the top level, so we start there. Users meet risk and impact as parts of the priority view, which is closer to how they would use it.

This is a first look, not a verdict. It has two jobs:

  1. Usability: can people read the priority view, trust it and say what they would do next?
  2. Discovery: use the view as a conversation starter to learn what people actually want to know about risk, so the risk slice is built from their needs, not our assumptions.

The second job is the main one. Spend roughly a third of each session on it.

What we are testing

The page shows five fictional priority 1 diseases. Each disease shows priority (band, rank, score out of 5), then threat (risk of incursion, impact) and preparedness (control measures, response options), each scored out of 5 and labelled very low, low, medium, high or very high. A plain-language summary is at the top. Below are the band key, how the score works and where the information comes from. The source is deliberately not repeated at the top, so T6 tests whether people find it at the bottom.

In scope: reading and comparing, the priority band and score, the four layers, the summary, sorting, source and trust. Out of scope: the disease page, the layer pages and how users route to them (the concept test). Clicks on those links land on a "This page is not part of the test" page. Record every one of them as a first click. They show where people expect to go next, which feeds the routing decision. Not being tested: whether the scores are right. All five scores are illustrative and out of 5, with no formula shown, so there is less to challenge. If SMEs question how a score is made, capture it, but don't debate it. See "Risks".

What has changed: the priority view shows change in priority only. Changes in risk, impact, control measures and response options belong on their own pages, which are not designed yet. The priority view shows two things: a "What has changed" paragraph in the summary, and an outlined tag ("Score increased", "Score decreased") with "from 4.4" under the score for diseases that changed. Unchanged diseases show nothing. The summary also says no disease changed rank or band, and links to the four measure pages for what drove the change.

Where the numbers come from: the scores are illustrative and out of 5. Himalayan Dragon Pox was 4.4 and is now 4.1. Wing wobble was 1.2 and is now 1.4. Ranks and bands are unchanged. The team should replace these if real previous scores exist.

Impact areas: impact shows the four areas the team gave: Public health, Animal welfare, Wider economy and society, International trade. A disease can have more than one, so they show as a short list under the level. Which disease has which area is made up for the prototype, so that all four appear.

Design options

Three versions are designed as prototypes with the same data and wording. See priority view options. An earlier matrix with diseases across the top was dropped: it isn't how people think about the problem and it pushed the summary down. - A: ranked table (open option A). Best for comparing one measure across diseases. - B: grouped by action (open option B). Best for "what do I do?", with a one-line reason for each disease. - C: table or cards (open option C). One page with a "View as" switch between A and B. The choice is kept in the address (?view=cards), sorting is kept when switching, and it starts on cards on small screens.

The summary is at the top in all three. The table has one header row, a light tint on the sorted column, and links that don't change colour once visited. Test the group labels in the "What we watch for" section.

How to run them (recommended): use C for everyone. It contains A and B, so all 6 participants see both. Start half on the table and half on cards. Watch whether they switch, when and why, then ask which they prefer. The cost is that C can't isolate A against B, because the first view colours what people do next. If the team needs a clean comparison, run A and B as separate pages, alternating across participants, and show the other for about 2 minutes at the end. Treat the preference answers as evidence, not a vote. Extra things to watch: in C, which view do they land on, do they find the switch without help, and do they switch for a reason (comparing a measure, briefing someone)? If one view is rarely used, drop the other. In B, do people open a card or just read the band? In A, is sorting found, and does the tint help them see which column is sorted? Sorting only applies to the table. Width: A uses a 1,200px container because six columns need about 1,000px. B uses the standard 960px. Test on a laptop first. A scrolls sideways below about 1,050px.

Research questions

# Question Needs
RQ1 Can users say which disease is the top priority, and why? UN4
RQ2 Do they understand priority as threat combined with preparedness, and that a high risk can still be low priority? UN4, UN9
RQ3 Do they tell risk of incursion and impact apart, and the preparedness layers apart? UN5, brief hypothesis 6
RQ4 Do they trust it, and do they know where the data came from? UN1
RQ5 What do the band names, score and wording mean to them? "Act now", "Prioritise", "Maintain", "Control measures", "Response options" wording
RQ6 What do they want to know about risk that this view doesn't tell them? discovery
RQ7 What would they do next, and where would they click? routing
RQ8 Do they notice what has changed in priority, and do they know where to go to find out why? UN2

Participants

  • 5 to 6 SMEs and policy or operational leads. Five finds most usability problems. The sixth helps with the discovery themes.
  • Mix people who work by disease and people who work by layer (for example IDM, impact modelling, preparedness). Record role and what they usually look at. This tells us whether role predicts what they want.
  • Include at least one assistive technology user if recruitment allows, and one person who has not seen the framework before.
  • Open question from the brief: can everyone open the IDM report link? Check at recruitment.

Session plan (50 minutes, 1:1, moderated)

Min Part What happens
0–5 Introduction Purpose, consent, recording. Say: "We're testing the page, not you. The data is illustrative and the diseases are fictional." Don't use "slice", "layer" or "framework"
5–12 How they work today "Tell me about the last time you had to decide which diseases to focus on." Which sources, what they rework by hand, who asks, what "risk" means to them
12–17 Unprompted first look Open the page. Think aloud, no task: "What is this? What is it telling you?" Stay quiet. Note what they read first and what they ignore
17–32 Tasks See the table below. Think aloud, no help
32–44 What do you want from this view? See "Discovery" below
44–50 Wrap up Preference and trust, "one thing you'd change", thanks

Run the first look and tasks before the discovery questions, so the prompts don't colour what people see.

Tasks

Scenario: "You've been asked to brief your director on which exotic diseases the team should focus on this month."

# Task Success looks like Maps to

T8 "What would you do next, and where would you look?" Says an action. Records the first click RQ7

Ask T9, T10 and T13 after T1 to T8, so the routes don't hint at the answers. The placeholder page shows the researcher exactly what was chosen. T13 needs the disease page, which is only an early idea, so it is a walkthrough on a mock-up until that page is designed.

Routes out of the priority view

Some users want more on one disease. Others want to compare one measure (risk, impact, control measures, response options) across all diseases. The page gives each group its own route, and keeps both separate from sorting.

Route Where it is Why
Disease Disease names in the table, the cards and the summary. A line under the table says "Select a disease name…" Names are what people already look for. Three places means a first click is rarely a miss
Measure Service navigation under the header: Priority, Risk, Impact, Control measures, Response options. Plus a "Look in more detail" line under the table with the same links in plain words The navigation is always there and is how people come back. The line is next to the table, where the question comes up

Moving across levels: people can cross from a measure page to a disease (select the disease name) and from a disease to a measure ("See all diseases on risk", impact, control measures or response options). The "Priority" link in the navigation gets them back, and returning keeps their view and sort. The full flow, with a journeys table, is in priority view options. The Priority page is still in design. The disease and Risk pages are only early ideas (see the wireframes), and Impact, Control measures and Response options aren't explored yet, so T13 and the cross-over links need a mock-up or a walkthrough.

Why not link the column headings? They already sort. Making one heading do both would hide one of the jobs. If the team prefers "table as the router" (concept 2 in the earlier proposal), sorting moves to a "Sort by" select above the table. That would also fix sorting on small screens, where it is hidden now. Test that as a variant, not as the default.

Labels: the navigation says Risk, Impact, Control measures and Response options, not "layer" or "slice". Watch what people call these and whether they understand that each one shows all diseases.

Not yet designed: the disease page and the four measure pages. The disease and Risk pages have very early ideas in the wireframes (1A to 1C for risk across diseases, 2A to 2C for one disease). Impact, Control measures and Response options aren't explored. Links lead to a placeholder that says what was chosen. Preparedness data isn't ready, so that page may need an empty state later.

Comprehension probes

Ask after the tasks, one at a time, and note the words they use. - "What does 'Act now' mean to you? Would you do something different for 'Prioritise'?" - "What do the numbers out of 5 tell you? Is a high number good or bad?" (Preparedness is higher = better, but a high risk is worse. The words very low to very high sit beside the numbers, so "High" means good for control measures and bad for risk. This is the main confusion risk.) - "What does a score of 4.1 out of 5 mean?" Do they open "How the priority score is worked out"? Do they find it helpful or too much? - "What is the difference between 'control measures' and 'response options'?" - "Is anything missing that you'd need before you trusted the order?"

Discovery: understanding what people want from risk

Use these after the tasks. Probe with "why?" and "how would you use that?".

  1. Open question. "If this page could answer one more question about risk, what would it be?"
  2. Decisions. "What decision would you make from this page? What do you need to decide it?"
  3. Risk, in their words. "When you say a disease is 'high risk', what do you mean? What would change your mind?"
  4. Information card sort. Give them cards (or a list to rank) of things we could show. Ask them to sort into must have, nice to have and don't need, and say why. - Whether the risk has changed, and which direction - Why it changed (reasons) - Risk over time (history) - When it was last assessed, and when it will be next - Source and who owns it - Where the disease is now (countries, routes of entry, season) - Which species or sectors are affected - Confidence in the assessment - Short operational summary - What other teams are doing - Add your own
  5. Scale. "Low, Medium, High: is that enough? What would you want in between?" (The workshop used five levels. The framework uses three.)
  6. Over time. "Would you want to know what has changed since last month? How would you want to see it?"
  7. Other views. "Would you ever want to start from risk, or impact, across all diseases instead of from the ranking?" (Only ask if they raise it. Otherwise it leads them.)

What we watch for

  • Hesitation or errors on T2, T3 and the number-out-of-10 probe. These are the likely misreadings.
  • Whether the colour carries the message. Do they use the band label, the colour, or both? Do they read the impact tag colour as priority?
  • Where eyes and cursor go first, and whether they scan across a row or down a column.
  • Words they use for the layers. These might replace "control measures" and "response options".
  • Whether sorting is found and used. It is only discoverable from the underlined headings.
  • Whether people miss the Threat and Preparedness group headings. The table has no group row, but rules separate priority, threat and preparedness. If people mix up the two groups, or ask which numbers are "good", bring a lighter version back.
  • Questions they ask the moderator. Each one is a gap in the page.
  • Disagreement with the ranking. Note who and why. It may be a data issue, a scoring issue or a legitimate need.

Success signals

Within a minute and unprompted, users can say: which disease is the top priority, why, which has the greatest impact, and where the information came from. They say what they'd do next. We also leave with a ranked list of what users want to know about risk, which becomes the brief for the risk slice.

How we'll decide

If we see Then
Users read the table and the bands correctly Keep the structure. Move on to the disease page and the risk slice
Users misread the preparedness numbers Test other wording or show "gap" instead of "score". Review the colour
Users ask "what changed?" repeatedly, even with the tags Move the reason for change onto the priority view, or add a "Latest changes" view (brief hypothesis 2)
Users ask "why did it change?" and can't find out The link from the summary to the measure pages isn't enough. Add the main reason next to the change, or open the disease page at the change
Users miss the change tags but find the summary line Keep the summary line and drop the tags, or make them stronger
Users over-react to a change that doesn't move a band Make the band effect clearer next to the change, and test the wording
Users go to a layer first (risk, impact) Feed into the routing concept test. Consider a more visible layer route
Users don't understand "control measures" or "response options" Test their own words. Keep the explanation under the heading
Users reject the ranking itself Separate the data from the model, and take it to the SMEs who own the scores
The card sort has clear "must haves" Those become the first version of the risk slice

Method and logistics

  • Format: moderated 1:1, in person or remote. 50 minutes. Audio and screen recorded with consent.
  • Prototype: this page, opened from a link or a laptop. Test on a laptop first. Small screens show a stacked list and sorting is hidden there, so test mobile only if the audience needs it.
  • Roles: one moderator, one note-taker. Observers are quiet and use a shared notes template (task, first click, success, quotes, issues, wants).
  • Analysis: after every 2 sessions, group notes into issues and "wants" on a wall. After all sessions, count how many users hit each issue and rate severity. Show the card sort as a ranked table.
  • Timing: 5 working days. Day 1 prepare and pilot (1 person), days 2 to 4 sessions, day 5 analyse and play back.

Risks

  • Testing the model, not the page. SMEs may want to debate the formula, bands or scores. Park it in a "model questions" list for the model owners and bring it back to the team. Keep the session on the view.
  • Illustrative data read as real. The page says so, and the moderator says it at the start. Disease names are fictional for the same reason.
  • Preparedness data isn't ready. The page shows it as if it were. Tell participants this is a design concept, and don't claim the numbers exist.
  • Leading the discovery. Don't show any further screens. Don't use "layer" or "slice". Only ask about other views if they raise them first.
  • Colour and accessibility. Test with at least one user who has low vision or colour vision difference if possible. See below.

Colour accessibility

The prototype uses the defra-frontend green theme (placeholder values, not yet approved by a Defra design authority) over GOV.UK Frontend. Checked against WCAG 2.2 AA with computed contrast ratios, plus an automated pass over every text and background pairing on the page (35 combinations, none failing, lowest 4.86:1).

Design decisions that follow: - Colour is never the only cue. Every band, risk level and impact level has a text label. Rank and score are printed. - Preparedness is not traffic-lit. A high number is good here and a high risk is bad, so red-to-green would send mixed signals and fails for red-green colour blindness. Bar length plus the number carries it. This is a design choice we should check in the test (H6). - Defra green is not used for text or header fills. A green header with white text would be 3.33:1. The header is black with a green stripe. - The page background is white. An earlier version used a pale green taken from the Defra theme's template-background setting (green tint-95). I had approximated the hex myself, and it isn't a brand-approved value. Panels use GOV.UK grey #f3f2f1. It is one variable if the team wants the tint back. - Colour pairs that need care for colour blindness: red and orange bands. They differ in lightness (white text on dark red, black text on light orange) and always carry a label. - Not yet checked: a real screen reader pass, 200% zoom and text-spacing checks, and forced-colours mode. The score bars are marked decorative (aria-hidden) and the table uses scope, colgroup and aria-sort. Do an accessibility review before testing with users. - If the Defra palette changes after the brand audit, re-run the ratios above.

Open decisions for the team

  1. Brand mode. The prototype uses Defra mode (system font, green links, text header) rather than GOV.UK mode (GDS Transport, crown header). The brief says GOV.UK only and the page title pattern ends "– GOV.UK". Which do we want in front of users?
  2. Who writes the summary, and how often? The prototype shows it as written copy.
  3. "Preparedness" or "vulnerability". The board says "vulnerability (preparedness)". The prototype says "Preparedness". Do we test both?
  4. Change is shown for priority only, as a summary line and as tags under the score (changed diseases only). Do we also show "No change" on every row? Showing only changes keeps the table calmer. The risk is that people can't tell "no change" from "not assessed". Risk, impact, control measures and response options changes, with reasons, belong on their own pages. Those pages aren't designed yet, so T12 will show where people expect to find the cause.
  5. Real or fictional disease names. The prototype uses fictional names, as the brief decided. SMEs may find it harder to judge plausibility.
  6. Which participants, and how do we recruit people who work by disease and by layer?
  7. How do people reach a measure view? The page offers the top navigation plus a line under the table. If T10 shows people miss both, test column headings as links, with sorting moved to a select.
  8. Control measures and response options are separate pages. The framework has both, so the navigation now shows two links, matching the table headings. Do people expect them together?
