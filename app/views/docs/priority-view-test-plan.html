Priority view test plan

Status: Draft for team review · Prototype: option C (updated 11 November 2026). Options A and B have not been updated and are now out of line with C. Supersedes the "risk slice on its own" test in earlier test proposal. Sources: design brief, prototype spec and the journeys and mapping board (5 Nov 2026).

Why this test, and why first

The team has decided not to test the risk slice on its own. Priority is the top level, so we start there. Users meet risk and impact as parts of the priority view, which is closer to how they would use it.

This is a first look, not a verdict. It has two jobs:

  1. Usability: can people read the priority view, trust it and say what they would do next?
  2. Discovery: use the view as a conversation starter to learn what people actually want to know about risk, so the risk slice is built from their needs, not our assumptions.

The second job is the main one. Spend roughly a third of each session on it.

What we are testing

The page shows six fictional priority 1 diseases, sorted by priority score with the highest first. Nothing is numbered. Each disease shows its priority (a priority action tag, the score out of 25, and the date it changed). Then it shows risk of incursion and impact as named levels (Rare to Almost certain, Insignificant to Severe), with the number hidden. Control measures and response options are checklists: a line such as "7 of 8 need review" and a bar with red segments for the items to review. Priority is risk × impact, out of 25. Control measures and response options sit beside the score and do not change it.

There is no summary at the top of the page and no paragraph explaining the scores above the list. Disease names are plain text, each with a "More details" link. For the two Act now diseases the link reads "Act now: see why this is a priority". In the cards view each disease has a one-line reason, and a "More details" link follows each of the four measures. At the bottom is a key ("What the priority actions mean" and "What the four measures mean"), then how the priority score is worked out and where the information comes from. The source is deliberately not repeated at the top, so T6 tests whether people find it at the bottom.

In scope: reading and comparing, the priority action and score, the four measures, the reason on each card, sorting, the confirmed-in-the-UK state, the change display, source and trust. Out of scope: the disease page, the measure pages and how users route to them (the concept test). Clicks on those links land on a "This page is not part of the test" page. Record every one of them as a first click. They show where people expect to go next, which feeds the routing decision. Not being tested: whether the scores are right. All six scores are illustrative. The formula is shown in "How the priority score is worked out", so some people may challenge it. If SMEs question how a score is made, or where the cut-offs sit, capture it, but don't debate it. The cadences, the checklist totals (10 control measure items and 8 response option items) and the priority action cut-offs are assumed. See "Risks".

What has changed: the priority view shows change in priority only. Changes in risk, impact, control measures and response options belong on their own pages, which are not designed yet. In the table, a changed score shows "Up from 12" or "Down from 16" under the score, with a small arrow at the end of that line. The words are always written and the arrow is decorative. On cards, a changed score shows "Previous score 12" and an outlined tag underneath ("Score increased" or "Score decreased"). The table uses the arrow and the cards use the tag because cards have more room. Every disease also shows "Changed [date]", so a stable score can be told apart from one that nobody has checked. We chose "Changed" over "Last changed" because it is shorter and means the same. Three diseases show a date but no "Up from" or "Down from" line. An earlier version had no arrows and showed nothing for unchanged diseases. Both decisions are reversed.

Where the numbers come from: the scores are illustrative. Sprouting hoof disease is 20 (Almost certain × Major, changed 6 October 2026). Scaly claw fever is 15 (Significant, up from 12, changed 3 November 2026). Himalayan Dragon Pox is 12 (Moderate × Major, down from 16, changed 5 November 2026). Rainbow tail disease is 10 (Unlikely × Severe, changed 12 May 2026). Fuzzy fox flu is 8 (Likely × Minor, changed 7 September 2026). Wing wobble is 2 (Rare × Minor, up from 1, changed 29 October 2026). So two changes cross a priority action: Scaly claw fever moves up into Act now and Himalayan Dragon Pox moves down out of it. The team should replace these if real previous scores exist.

Confirmed in the UK: Scaly claw fever is the sixth disease and shows a "Confirmed in the UK" state in place of a risk level. It is a purple tag with "Since 3 November 2026. 2 premises." underneath. It is one of the two Act now diseases. How a confirmed case is rated for the score is not decided. The score of 15 is made up.

Impact areas: impact shows the four areas the team gave: Public health, Animal welfare, Wider economy and society, International trade. A disease can have more than one, so they show as a short list under the level. Which disease has which area is made up for the prototype, so that all four appear.

Design options

Three versions are designed as prototypes. See priority view options. Only option C has been updated to the final state described on this page. Options A and B still show the earlier five diseases, scores out of 5, ranks and bands, so they are out of line with C. Update them before using them in a session. An earlier matrix with diseases across the top was dropped: it isn't how people think about the problem and it pushed the content down. - A: ranked table (open option A). Best for comparing one measure across diseases. Not updated. - B: grouped by action (open option B). Best for "what do I do?", with a one-line reason for each disease. Not updated. - C: table or cards (open option C). One page with a "View as" switch between a table and cards. The choice is kept in the address (?view=cards), sorting is kept when switching, and it starts on cards on small screens. The table has six columns: Disease, Priority, Risk of incursion, Impact, Control measures and Response options. Each heading has a short hint (for example "Risk × impact, out of 25" and "Chance of arriving in the UK"). The Disease column is wide enough that the longest name does not wrap. The Priority cell holds the tag, the score, any change and the "Changed [date]" line. The cards are grouped by priority action and sit two across at 64em and wider. Each card shows "Priority score 20 out of 25", the change, the one-line reason, a "More details" link and the four measures. Decision pages for each choice are in app/assets/design/priority-view/, served at /public/design/priority-view/: priority-cell-options.html, score-change-options.html, checklist-cell-options.html, risk-impact-bars-options.html and card-hint-options.html.

Option C has no summary at the top, no paragraph explaining the scores above the list, and no "Look in more detail" section. The four measure pages are still reached from the service navigation. The table has one header row, a light tint on the sorted column, and links that don't change colour once visited. Risk and impact have no grey bars, to see whether they are missed. Watch how people use the priority action tags and the key at the bottom.

How to run them (recommended): use C for everyone. It has the table and the cards on one page, so all 6 participants see both. Start half on the table and half on cards. Watch whether they switch, when and why, then ask which they prefer. The cost is that C can't isolate the table against the cards, because the first view colours what people do next. If the team needs a clean comparison with A and B, update them first, then run them as separate pages, alternating across participants, and show the other for about 2 minutes at the end. Treat the preference answers as evidence, not a vote. Extra things to watch: in C, which view do they land on, do they find the switch without help, and do they switch for a reason (comparing a measure, briefing someone)? If one view is rarely used, drop the other. In the cards view, do people open a card or just read the priority action? In the table, is sorting found, and does the tint help them see which column is sorted? Sorting only applies to the table. Width: C uses a wide container because six columns need about 1,000px. Test on a laptop first. The table scrolls sideways on narrow screens.

Research questions

# Question Needs

RQ2 Do they understand that priority comes from risk and impact, that control measures and response options sit beside it without changing the score, and that a low risk can still be a priority if the impact is severe? UN4, UN9
RQ3 Do they tell risk of incursion and impact apart, and control measures and response options apart? UN5, brief hypothesis 6
RQ4 Do they trust it, and do they know where the data came from? UN1
RQ5 What do the priority action names, the score and the wording mean to them? "Act now", "Prioritise", "Monitor", "Maintain", "Control measures", "Response options", "need review" and the impact names such as "Minor" wording
RQ6 What do they want to know about risk that this view doesn't tell them? discovery
RQ7 What would they do next, and where would they click? routing
RQ8 Do they notice what has changed in priority, read "Changed [date]" correctly, and know where to go to find out why? UN2
RQ9 Do they read "N of M need review" and the red bars the right way round, and how do they think control measures and response options link to priority now that they do not change the score? wording, UN9

Participants

  • 5 to 6 SMEs and policy or operational leads. Five finds most usability problems. The sixth helps with the discovery themes.
  • Mix people who work by disease and people who work by layer (for example IDM, impact modelling, preparedness). Record role and what they usually look at. This tells us whether role predicts what they want.
  • Include at least one assistive technology user if recruitment allows, and one person who has not seen the framework before.
  • Open question from the brief: can everyone open the IDM report link? Check at recruitment.

Session plan (50 minutes, 1:1, moderated)

Min Part What happens
0–5 Introduction Purpose, consent, recording. Say: "We're testing the page, not you. The data is illustrative and the diseases are fictional." Don't use "slice", "layer" or "framework"
5–12 How they work today "Tell me about the last time you had to decide which diseases to focus on." Which sources, what they rework by hand, who asks, what "risk" means to them

Run the first look and tasks before the discovery questions, so the prompts don't colour what people see.

Routes out of the priority view

Some users want more on one disease. Others want to compare one measure (risk, impact, control measures, response options) across all diseases. The page gives each group its own route, and keeps both separate from sorting.

Route Where it is Why
Disease A "More details" link under the name in the table, and after the summary on each card. The disease name itself is plain text. For the two Act now diseases the link reads "Act now: see why this is a priority" The link text says where it goes. We are not sure "what to do now" content exists, so the wording doesn't promise actions or disease facts. Watch whether people still try to click the name (T9)
Measure Service navigation under the header: Priority, Risk, Impact, Control measures, Response options. On cards, a "More details" link follows each of the four measures, with hidden text that names the measure and the disease The navigation is always there and is how people come back. The "Look in more detail" section under the table was removed, so the navigation is now the only route to one measure across all diseases. The card links go to one measure for one disease

Moving across levels: people can cross from a measure page to a disease (by a link on the disease) and from a disease to a measure ("See all diseases on risk", impact, control measures or response options). The "Priority" link in the navigation gets them back, and returning keeps their view and sort. The full flow, with a journeys table, is in priority view options. That page was written before option C changed, so it may still say "select a disease name" and mention "Look in more detail". The Priority page is still in design. The disease and Risk pages are only early ideas (see the wireframes), and Impact, Control measures and Response options aren't explored yet, so T13 and the cross-over links need a mock-up or a walkthrough.

Why not link the column headings? They already sort. Making one heading do both would hide one of the jobs. If the team prefers "table as the router" (concept 2 in the earlier proposal), sorting moves to a "Sort by" select above the table. That would also fix sorting on small screens, where it is hidden now. Test that as a variant, not as the default.

Labels: the navigation says Risk, Impact, Control measures and Response options, not "layer" or "slice". Watch what people call these and whether they understand that each one shows all diseases.

Not yet designed: the disease page and the four measure pages. The disease and Risk pages have very early ideas in the wireframes (1A to 1C for risk across diseases, 2A to 2C for one disease). Impact, Control measures and Response options aren't explored. Links lead to a placeholder that says what was chosen. Preparedness data isn't ready, so that page may need an empty state later.

Comprehension probes

Ask after the tasks, one at a time, and note the words they use. Say "priority action" or just the names. Don't say "band". - "What does 'Act now' mean to you? Would you do something different for 'Prioritise'?" - "Minor". "Here is the impact scale: Insignificant, Minor, Significant, Major, Severe. Where would you put 'Minor'? What would a minor impact mean in practice? Is it nothing, or a small amount of harm?" Ask the same for "Rare" and "Almost certain" if there is time. - "What does a score of 12 out of 25 tell you? How would you say it is worked out?" Do they open "How the priority score is worked out"? Do they find it helpful or too much? - "Control measures". "What do you think 'Control measures' means? What would be on the checklist?" Then: "What is the difference between 'control measures' and 'response options'?" - "N of M need review". Point at "9 of 10 need review". "Is that good or bad? What would you do about it?" Note whether a long red bar reads as bad, as "full", or as well covered. Ask whether the checklists change the priority score. - "More details". "Where do you think this link would take you? What would you expect to find there?" Ask for one in the table and one on a card. - "Changed [date]". Point at "Changed 6 October 2026" under a score with no "Up from" line. "What does this tell you? Did the score change, or did someone check it?" Then ask what else they would want to know, for example how long it has been since the score was checked. - "Act now: see why this is a priority". "What do you expect to find behind this link? Would you expect to see actions, or reasons?" - The plain-text name. Don't ask. Watch whether anyone tries to click the disease name, and note what they say when nothing happens. - The key. "How would you find out what 'Prioritise' means?" Do they find "What the priority actions mean" at the bottom? Do they use it, and do they find it helpful? - Small screens. If you test on a phone or a narrow window: "Is any of the text hard to read?" Some text is 14px there, such as the labels on cards. See "Colour accessibility". - "Is anything missing that you'd need before you trusted the order?" - "Would a short summary at the top of the page help you? What would it need to say for your role?" (Ask last, so it doesn't prompt them earlier.)

Discovery: understanding what people want from risk

Use these after the tasks. Probe with "why?" and "how would you use that?".

  1. Open question. "If this page could answer one more question about risk, what would it be?"
  2. Decisions. "What decision would you make from this page? What do you need to decide it?"
  3. Risk, in their words. "When you say a disease is 'high risk', what do you mean? What would change your mind?"
  4. Information card sort. Give them cards (or a list to rank) of things we could show. Ask them to sort into must have, nice to have and don't need, and say why. - Whether the risk has changed, and which direction - Why it changed (reasons) - Risk over time (history) - When it was last assessed, and when it will be next - Source and who owns it - Where the disease is now (countries, routes of entry, season) - Which species or sectors are affected - Confidence in the assessment - Short operational summary - What other teams are doing - Add your own
  5. Scale. "Low, Medium, High: is that enough? What would you want in between?" (The workshop used five levels. The framework uses three.)
  6. Over time. "Would you want to know what has changed since last month? How would you want to see it?"
  7. Other views. "Would you ever want to start from risk, or impact, across all diseases instead of from the priority order?" (Only ask if they raise it. Otherwise it leads them.)

What we watch for

  • Hesitation or errors on T2, T3 and the "need review" probe. These are the likely misreadings.
  • Whether the colour carries the message. Do they use the priority action name, the colour, or both? Do they read the impact tag colour as priority?
  • Where eyes and cursor go first, and whether they scan across a row or down a column.
  • Words they use for the four measures. These might replace "control measures" and "response options".
  • Whether sorting is found and used. It is only discoverable from the underlined headings.
  • Whether people read control measures and response options as part of the score. The table has no group row. Note anyone who says a disease is high priority because we are poorly prepared, or low because we are well prepared. That reads preparedness into the score, which it does not change.
  • Whether people try to click the plain-text disease name, and whether they find the "More details" link without help.
  • Whether people find the key at the bottom and use it instead of asking the moderator.
  • Screen reader users. In the table, the "More details" link sits inside the row header, so a screen reader may read it with each cell in the row. Test it with an assistive technology user if we have one.
  • Questions they ask the moderator. Each one is a gap in the page.
  • Disagreement with the order. Note who and why. It may be a data issue, a scoring issue or a legitimate need.

Success signals

Within a minute and unprompted, users can say: which diseases are Act now and which comes first by score, why, which has the greatest impact, which is already in the UK, and where the information came from. They read "N of M need review" the right way round, and say what "Changed [date]" means. They say what they'd do next. We also leave with a prioritised list of what users want to know about risk, which becomes the brief for the risk slice.

Method and logistics

  • Format: moderated 1:1, in person or remote. 50 minutes. Audio and screen recorded with consent.
  • Prototype: this page, opened from a link or a laptop. Test on a laptop first. Small screens start on cards and sorting is hidden there, so test mobile only if the audience needs it. If you do, note the 14px text.
  • Roles: one moderator, one note-taker. Observers are quiet and use a shared notes template (task, first click, success, quotes, issues, wants).
  • Analysis: after every 2 sessions, group notes into issues and "wants" on a wall. After all sessions, count how many users hit each issue and rate severity. Show the card sort as a ranked table.
  • Timing: 5 working days. Day 1 prepare and pilot (1 person), days 2 to 4 sessions, day 5 analyse and play back.

Risks

  • Testing the model, not the page. SMEs may want to debate the formula, cut-offs or scores. The formula is now shown, so this is more likely. Park it in a "model questions" list for the model owners and bring it back to the team. Keep the session on the view.
  • Illustrative data read as real. The page says so, and the moderator says it at the start. Disease names are fictional for the same reason.
  • Preparedness data isn't ready. The page shows it as if it were, as checklists with assumed totals. Tell participants this is a design concept, and don't claim the data exists.
  • Leading the discovery. Don't show any further screens. Don't use "layer" or "slice". Only ask about other views if they raise them first.
  • Colour and accessibility. Test with at least one user who has low vision or colour vision difference if possible. See below.

Design decisions that follow: - Colour is never the only cue. Every priority action, risk level and impact level has a text label. The score is printed, and the checklists print the count. - Checklists have red only, no green. More items to review is worse, the same direction as risk and impact, so no figure needs to be read the opposite way. Red against empty, not red against green, carries the meaning, which also works for red-green colour blindness. This is a design choice we should check in the test (H6). - Defra green is not used for text or header fills. A green header with white text would be 3.33:1. The header is black with a green stripe. - The page background is white. An earlier version used a pale green taken from the Defra theme's template-background setting (green tint-95). I had approximated the hex myself, and it isn't a brand-approved value. Panels use GOV.UK grey #f3f2f1. It is one variable if the team wants the tint back. - Colour pairs that need care for colour blindness: red and orange priority action tags. They differ in lightness (white text on dark red, black text on light orange) and always carry a label. - Text size on small screens: some text is 14px on small screens (GOV.UK body-s, for example the labels on cards). Desktop uses 16px as the floor. Check it with a low vision user and decide whether to raise it. - Not yet checked: a real screen reader pass, 200% zoom and text-spacing checks, and forced-colours mode (the checklist bars are drawn as backgrounds, so they may not show). The checklist bars and arrows are marked decorative (aria-hidden) and the table uses scope and aria-sort. In the table the "More details" link is inside the row header, so check how a screen reader reads each row. Do an accessibility review before testing with users. - If the Defra palette changes after the brand audit, re-run the ratios above.

Open decisions for the team

  1. Brand mode. The prototype uses Defra mode (system font, green links, text header) rather than GOV.UK mode (GDS Transport, crown header). The brief says GOV.UK only and the page title pattern ends "– GOV.UK". Which do we want in front of users?
  2. Does anyone also need a summary at the top of the page? We removed it because it had to cover every section, so it was too long, and one summary can't be both short and right for everyone. The card reasons stay. Listen for who asks for a top summary, what role they have and what they would want it to say. If they do, it may need to be per role. Also ask who would write the card reasons, and how often. The prototype shows them as written copy.
  3. "Preparedness" or "vulnerability". The board says "vulnerability (preparedness)". Option C no longer shows a group name above control measures and response options. Do we need one, and which word do we test?
  4. Change is shown for priority only, as an arrow in the table and a tag on cards, and every row shows "Changed [date]". Do we need a tag or a date for how long a score has stood? Risk, impact, control measures and response options changes, with reasons, belong on their own pages. Those pages aren't designed yet, so T12 will show where people expect to find the cause.
  5. Real or fictional disease names. The prototype uses fictional names, as the brief decided. SMEs may find it harder to judge plausibility.
  6. Which participants, and how do we recruit people who work by disease and by layer?
  7. How do people reach a measure view? The page offers the service navigation, and the card links go to one measure for one disease. The "Look in more detail" section was removed. If T10 shows people miss the navigation, test column headings as links, with sorting moved to a select.
  8. Control measures and response options are separate pages. The framework has both, so the navigation now shows two links, matching the table headings. Do people expect them together?
  9. How does preparedness link into priority? Control measures and response options no longer change the score. How should the view show that they matter, and when?
  10. What is assumed? Real update cadences, the checklist item totals (10 and 8) and the priority action cut-offs are assumed and need real values.
  11. What does "Changed" mean? Is it that the score value changed, or that the inputs were last checked? Is a change tag or a date for how long needed?
  12. Is there "what to do now" content behind the Act now link? Does the destination page match "see why this is a priority"?
  13. Row header with a link. The table row header now includes the "More details" link, so a screen reader reads it on each cell of the row. Test it.
  14. Do people try to click the disease name now it is plain text?
  15. Is a short disease summary wanted in the Disease cell of the table? It fits, but makes the table about 20% taller and repeats on each row for screen readers. It has not been added.
  16. 14px text on small screens. GOV.UK body-s is 14px on small screens, under the 16px floor used on desktop. Do we raise it?
  17. Internal data source names in a public repository. The prototype and these documents name internal reports and indices, and the repository is public. This has not been confirmed as OK. Check before sharing the link or the repository more widely.
  18. Options A and B. They are out of line with C. Do we update them, or retire them and keep C?
  19. Decision pages. Five decision pages in app/assets/design/priority-view/ record the choices for the priority cell, checklists, risk and impact bars, card hints and score change. They are not yet linked from the home page.
