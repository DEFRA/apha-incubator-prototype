After the team design review

12 November 2026. The team reviewed the priority view. These changes make priority view prototype v1.1, in two versions. Every change below is in both versions. The only difference is how preparedness is shown. The test leads with combined preparedness, and the separate version is to show if combined is flagged as not working for people. More updates from the review will be added here.

The team chose option 1 of signalling priority without prescribing action. All the other wording that told readers what to do was removed too. See signalling priority

The separate version stays ready as the alternative. The combined page is the main entry point (index.html) and the separate page is separate.html
Preparedness is tested two ways. Control measures and response options stay as two separate columns, and are also combined into one preparedness measure with five statuses: Totally prepared, Largely prepared, Moderately prepared, Slightly prepared and Not at all prepared We do not yet know whether people need prevention and response shown separately, or only an overall status. The combined version replaces the count of items with a status. Each is its own screen, and they link to each other
The priority score combines all four measures The priority scoring team member said priority should reflect all four columns, not only risk × impact. The prototype uses a fictional process that mirrors the earlier one and adds both preparedness measures: threat (risk × impact) multiplied by 1 plus a preparedness gap, out of 60. The real scoring will be worked out later. The design only needs to show that all four are considered
Risk of incursion has six levels: Very high, High, Medium, Low, Very low and Negligible These replace the earlier names (Rare to Almost certain). A sixth colour, orange for Medium, was added so each level has its own

The change display is removed: the "Changed" date, the previous score, the arrows and the increased and decreased tags It rested on too many assumptions about what change matters. Research will ask what change people want to see, why, and what they would do with it, and we will design from there. The earlier options are kept on showing when a priority score changed

Knock-on changes: the priority levels now use cut-offs out of 60 (30, 20 and 8), the key and the "how the score is worked out" panel were rewritten, and the reasons on the cards were reworded to match the new level names. The decisions are in the design decisions.

Signalling priority without prescribing action

Decided 12 November 2026. Raised in the team design review. The team chose option 1, and it is in prototype v1.1.

The priority actions (Act now, Prioritise, Monitor and Maintain) are not very helpful at an individual level. Some people need to act on a disease and others don’t, depending on their role, so telling everyone to "Act now" wrongly attributes action. But the column still has to get attention, because the CEO does need to organise action. We came up with eight ways to signal priority that do not prescribe an action that is not relevant to everyone.

Options for signalling priority without prescribing action
Option The idea
1. Say where it sits Keep the coloured tags, but say Highest, High, Medium or Low priority instead of an action
2. Numbered priority groups A neutral label, Priority group 1 to 4, with a key that describes scores and not action
3. A score bar No category. The score and a single-colour bar, so the longest bars draw the eye
4. Emphasise only the top group Only the highest scores get a heavy edge and a label. Everything else shows just the score
5. Show what is driving the score "Driven by: very high risk, not at all prepared", so each role sees whether their part is involved
6. A marker for leadership attention A separate "For leadership attention" marker on the top group. It names who needs to organise action and doesn’t tell everyone else to act
7. A view for your role People pick a role and the column signals what matters to that role. Needs the real roles, from research
8. Just the score, styled by band No categorisation language. Each disease shows its score, coloured by group. We ask users what they would call the groups and what they would group by, and the language comes from them

The team chose option 1, say where it sits. The tags now say Highest priority, High priority, Medium priority and Low priority instead of Act now, Prioritise, Monitor and Maintain. The colours and the cut-offs (30, 20 and 8 out of 60) are unchanged. We also removed the other wording that told readers what to do: the action sentences in the key and under the card groups, "Keep watching" and "maintain that readiness" in the card reasons, and "need action first" in the page intro. The link for the highest-priority disease now says "More details", like the others. Its hidden text for screen readers names the disease and says it is the highest priority. A higher score now means a higher priority, and says nothing about who should act.

Option 8 (just the score, styled by band) was rejected. Numbers and colour alone could confuse people who can’t see the colour, because nothing else says which way the scale runs, and they could mistake the direction of the scoring. A word carries the direction without needing colour. The risk with option 1 is that "Highest priority" may still be read as an instruction, and that "High" and "Medium" are also risk levels. Research should check both. The other options are kept on the options page, and could be added later, for example the drivers (option 5) or a marker for leadership attention (option 6).

Accessibility review before round 1

Reviewed 16 November 2026. Priority view prototype v1.1, both preparedness versions, table and cards views.

Result: no failures in the page content, and a short list of small enhancements. The design has been agreed with the team, so none of these is applied. They can go into the next round. We don’t expect to test with people who have specific access needs in this round.

How it was checked

  • Automated checks with axe-core 4.14 against WCAG 2.2 AA and best practice, on both versions, in table and cards views, at desktop (1300px) and phone (390px) widths.
  • Keyboard: tabbing through the whole page, checking order and focus visibility.
  • Reflow at 200% and 400% zoom (640px and 320px wide), text spacing, forced colours mode and link target sizes.
  • Not covered: a screen reader, voice control or magnification, and testing with people who have access needs. These checks do not replace them.

What passes

  • No automated failures in the page content. The only flags are the prototype’s own header bar and the phase banner sitting outside a landmark.
  • Keyboard: a logical order, a skip link, no traps, and every control shows a clear focus style. The view switch has a pressed state, and sorting is announced.
  • Colour: every text pair is at least 4.5:1 (the secondary text on the tinted column is 6.3:1). The bar outline is 4.6:1. Colour is never the only cue, because every level has a word and every count is printed.
  • Reflow and spacing: no horizontal scroll or lost content at 200% or 400% zoom, and nothing clipped with increased text spacing.
  • Names and structure: a sensible heading order, a page title and language, and unique link names. Each "More details" link says which disease or measure it is for.

Enhancements for the next round

Roughly in order of value. Each is a small change, and none changes the design the team agreed.

Accessibility enhancements for the next round
Finding Enhancement
Sortable headings are links that act as buttons. A screen reader announces "link", but it sorts and doesn’t navigate Use a button inside the heading, as the MOJ sortable table does. Keep aria-sort and the live announcement
In forced colours (high contrast) mode the tags lose their background and outline, and the red segments of the checklist bar vanish, leaving an empty box. The sort arrow becomes a dash. Nothing is lost, because the words and counts remain, but the bar looks empty Add a forced-colours style that draws the tags and bar segments with system colours and a border, and draws the sort arrow with currentcolor
On small screens the table turns into stacked blocks. That drops the table structure for some screen readers. We already start small screens on cards, but the Table button is still there Hide the Table button on small screens, or keep the table roles on the stacked version
The "More details" links are 18 to 20px high, and the navigation and footer links are 18 to 22px. WCAG 2.2 asks for 24px, and these pass only because they are spaced apart Add a little vertical padding so each link is at least 24px high
The key that explains the levels is at the bottom of the page. People who magnify the screen or find scales hard to hold in mind have to scroll to it Add a short "What the levels mean" link under the view switch that jumps to the key. This also helps with the question about the key’s position

It found the three problems listed under "Fixed after the review", which the automated checks had missed. Manual check yet to cover voice control, or NVDA on Windows. The table above is left as it was when the review was written. The scroll area tab stop in it has since been fixed.

Fixed after the review

  • Reader mode ran words together. Found on 16 November 2026 in Chrome’s reader mode, which drops the styles. A status and the link after it read as one line, for example "Not at all preparedMore details about preparedness for Sprouting hoof disease". The page used CSS alone to put them on separate lines. Our first attempts used block-level divs, and Chrome’s reader mode then dropped content: first the per-measure "More details" links (a block that holds only a link is treated as navigation), then the whole list of diseases, because divs full of short text made the key look like the main article. The fix that works keeps the original inline elements and puts a single line break between the pieces. The breaks are hidden by CSS, so the page looks identical, with no difference in screenshots of either version or view. With the styles removed, reader mode now keeps the list, the key and every link, each status and link on its own line. We checked this with Mozilla’s Readability, whose results matched the three behaviours seen in Chrome. We have not run Chrome’s reader mode itself. The review above did not test reader mode, and it is now part of the checks.
  • Table cells did not say which column they belong to. Found on 16 November 2026 using VoiceOver. Moving cell by cell, you hear only "Medium" and have to remember the column heading. Each table cell now starts with visually hidden text giving its column, for example "Risk of incursion: Medium" and "Impact: Major". Nothing changes visually, and on small screens, where the stacked layout already shows the label, the hidden text is switched off so it is not repeated. To check: VoiceOver may now say the column twice in some reading modes, once from the table headings and once from the hidden text. If it is too wordy, the option is to keep the hidden text and accept it, or to drop it and rely on the table headings. Listen to it and decide.
  • At 200% zoom the table was cut off at the right. Found on 16 November 2026 on a desktop screen. The table needs about 1000px, but at 200% zoom a desktop window is only 640 to 960px wide in CSS pixels. The table scrolled sideways, with no sign that more was there, and keyboard and voice users could not easily reach the hidden columns. The table now becomes a block per disease, like it does on a phone, whenever the window is narrower than 1056px, so there is no sideways scroll at any width. We checked widths from 320px to 1280px. The trade-off is that sorting is not available in that layout, and the note above the table says so. We also took the invisible column headings out of that layout, because their sort links were keyboard stops that could not be seen. The scroll wrapper is now a keyboard stop and a landmark only when the table really overflows, and a 15px page-wide sideways scroll between 1020px and 1260px is fixed. To check in research: whether anyone needs to sort while zoomed in.

What we have learned from designing

These are design learnings from making and comparing options. They are not research findings. Research learnings from each round will be added under the round.

Design learnings
Learning Where it led
One summary at the top can’t be short and right for everyone We removed the top summary and kept a one-line reason on each card
A rank number gave false precision We removed the rank and sort by priority score
Hints under every label made the cards busy A key at the bottom of the page explains the measures once. See card hint options
Red against green fails for colour blindness, and a high risk and a high preparedness score pull in opposite directions Control measures and response options show a count of items to review with dark red segments. See checklist options
Showing a change needs a date. Otherwise a stable score looks the same as one nobody has checked Every score says when it changed. See showing when a score changed
A tag, a score and a change are too much to read as three styles Plain lines in one style under the tag. See priority cell options
"Bands" is a word we made up, and capitals are harder to read "Priority actions", in sentence case
Link wording promises something. "Disease details" reads as facts about the disease, and "what to do now" promises content we may not have "Act now: see why this is a priority" for the two Act now diseases, and "More details" for the rest
A label that tells people what to do is wrong for people who don’t need to do it The priority actions were replaced by priority levels (decided). Eight ways to signal priority without prescribing action are in signalling priority
Showing change needs more than a date, and we were guessing what change matters After the team design review we removed it, and will ask users what change they want surfaced and why
A long date line squeezed the most important column, the disease name The Disease column has a minimum width and the date sits at the bottom of the cell in smaller text

Learning about how we are working with the AI design and coding agent

The questions for user research in the handover notes were drafted by the AI agent we use to design and build the prototype. That worked because we gave it a lot of design context, thinking and considerations as we refined the design, through the prompts we wrote. The researcher found the documentation really helpful.

The same approach has a cost. As the context builds up, the agent can get slower while we build the design in small steps and update the documentation as we go, and it uses more credits. Some regular work to keep the context small and current may be needed.

The nine user needs

The user needs we agreed on 26 October 2026 were twelve, in three themes. On 9 November we combined three pairs that overlapped, giving nine needs in the same three themes. The evidence behind each need is held by the user research team and is not published here.

  • UN1 One trusted source combines the earlier "One trusted source" and "One shared source". Both described one place everyone trusts that tells them when something changes.
  • UN2 Early warning signal combines "When risk changes" and "Agreed trigger point". The signal is in scope. Agreeing what happens when it changes, and who acts, is organisational, so that part sits with governance.
  • UN8 Right level of detail combines "Short operational summary" and "Right level of detail". Both were about getting information at the depth each role needs.

The other six carry across unchanged and are renumbered.

Wider open questions

These cut across rounds. Questions about one part are on that part’s page: priority view and risk exploration.

  • Brand. The prototype uses a Defra look: system font, green links, and a black header with a green stripe. The brief says GOV.UK only. Which do we put in front of users?
  • "Preparedness" or "vulnerability"? The scoring framework uses "vulnerability". The prototype says "Preparedness". Do we test both?
  • Real data. Which diseases have which impact areas, and what were the previous scores and change dates? All of it is made up in the prototype.
  • Internal source names. The prototype names internal data sources (IDM, D2R2, Risk ID and the preparedness indices) in "Where this information comes from". This is a public repository. Is that fine, or should we generalise them before the next push?
