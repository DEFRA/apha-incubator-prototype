Priority view design test · For the design team, not for participants

Signalling priority without prescribing action

The priority column tells people to Act now, Prioritise, Monitor or Maintain. Not everyone who reads the page needs to act, so these are options for signalling priority that don’t.

To review Not applied to prototype v1.1, which still uses the priority actions. These are options for the team to review.

The problem

The team review raised that the priority actions are not very helpful at an individual level. Some people will need to act on a disease and others won’t, depending on their role. Telling every user to "Act now" wrongly attributes action to everyone.

But the column still has to get attention. The CEO does need to organise action, and the highest priorities should stand out for them. So the design has to do two things at once:

  • Draw attention to the highest priorities, especially for a leader who organises action.
  • Not prescribe an action that is not relevant to everyone who reads it.

Principles for the options

  • Describe where a disease sits. Don’t say what to do about it.
  • Get attention through order, emphasis and size, not through commands.
  • Let each role find their own part, rather than guessing it for them.
  • Colour is never the only cue, so every option has a text label or a printed score.
  • The score stays visible in every option.

The mock-ups show only the disease name and the priority cell, with the same five fictional diseases as prototype v1.1. The other columns are unchanged. The score is out of 60 and the top group is 30 or more.

The options

  • 1. Say where it sits
  • 2. Numbered priority groups
  • 3. A score bar, with no category
  • 4. Emphasise only the top group
  • 5. Show what is driving the score
  • 6. A marker for leadership attention
  • 7. A view for your role
  • 8. Just the score, styled by band
  • How they compare, and ways to combine them

Option 1: say where it sits

Keep the four coloured tags, but change the words from an instruction to a position: Highest priority, High priority, Medium priority and Low priority. Same colours, same cut-offs.

Good for

  • The smallest change, because the colours, scale and layout stay as they are
  • Still draws the eye to the top of the list, so a leader sees it first
  • Says nothing about who should do what

Watch for

  • "Highest priority" may still be read as "I must act"
  • The word "priority" repeats in a column that is already called Priority
  • "High" and "Medium" are also risk levels, so they could be confused

Option 2: numbered priority groups

Use a neutral label, Priority group 1 to 4, with group 1 the highest. The key says what each group means in score terms, and nothing about action.

Good for

  • Completely neutral wording, with no verb
  • Familiar from other prioritisation schemes, and easy to refer to in a meeting
  • Keeps the colours that help a leader find the top

Watch for

  • Needs a key, and 1 is the highest while a higher score is also higher, which can confuse
  • Numbers can look like a rank, which we removed for giving false precision
  • Less meaning at a glance than a word

Option 3: a score bar, with no category

Drop the categories altogether. Each disease shows its score and a single-colour bar, filled in proportion to the score out of 60. The list is already sorted, so the longest bars are at the top.

Good for

  • No label that sounds like an instruction, and no role is implied
  • Shows how far apart the scores are, which a category hides
  • Needs no key, and only one colour

Watch for

  • No clear line between "needs attention" and "doesn’t", so a leader has to judge it
  • Loses the four groups on the cards view, which are grouped by priority action today
  • A bar is decoration, so the printed score has to carry the meaning

Option 4: emphasise only the top group

Don’t label every disease. Only the diseases in the top group (a score of 30 or more) get emphasis: a heavy red edge on the row and an outlined "Highest priority" label. Everything else shows just the score.

Good for

  • The strongest pull on a leader’s attention, because only the top stands out
  • Nobody else is told to do anything, and the lower rows stay calm
  • Fewer labels and less colour on the page

Watch for

  • Unlabelled rows may look unimportant, when a role could still care about them
  • The line for "top" (30 or more) is made up, and sets who gets noticed
  • With one disease in the top group, there may be little to see for most people

Option 5: show what is driving the score

Say why a disease scores as it does, so each role can see whether it is their part. A measure counts as a driver when it is at the worse end: a high or very high risk, a major or severe impact, or slightly or not at all prepared. This does not say who should act. The reader works that out.

Good for

  • Lets different roles find their own part without us guessing it, for example risk, impact or preparedness
  • Explains the score, which supports trust in it
  • No instruction at all

Watch for

  • Gives a leader no single attention signal, so it works better combined with another option
  • More text in the cell, and the cell is already busy
  • Needs a rule for "driver", and the rule here is made up

Option 6: a marker for leadership attention

Keep the score neutral, and add one separate marker that says who it is for: "For leadership attention". It appears on the diseases in the top group. It says that someone needs to organise action, and attributes that to leadership, not to every reader.

Good for

  • Gets the CEO’s attention directly, and says who it is for
  • Other readers see it informs leadership, and are not told to act
  • Separates "how high is the score" from "who needs to organise action"

Watch for

  • It names a role, so we need to be sure that is who organises action
  • Could read as "this is not for me" to someone who does need to act
  • The line for the marker (30 or more) is made up

Option 7: a view for your role

A bigger idea. People choose a role, and the Priority column signals what matters to that role, so nobody is shown a signal that is not for them. The roles below are examples only. The real roles would come from research.

Good for

  • The only option that tailors the signal, so each person sees what is relevant to them
  • Leaves the all-roles view completely neutral

  • More to build and explain, and a role choice can hide things people need
  • People may work across roles

Option 8: just the score, styled by band

Use no categorisation language at all. Each disease shows only its score. Behind the scenes the scores still fall into groups, but the groups only change how the score is styled: a colour on the score itself. We don’t name the groups. We ask users what they would call them, and what they would group by, and let the language come from them.

This is different from option 3. The score is the signal in both, but here the groups still exist, so a leader can see the top group at a glance, and the colours are the only grouping we offer.

Good for

  • No categorisation language to get wrong. Users give us the words, rather than us choosing them
  • Still catches a leader’s eye, because the colour on the top group stands out
  • Nobody is told to do anything, and the score stays the most important thing
  • Easy to add words later, once research tells us what people call the groups

Watch for

  • Colour carries the grouping, and there is no text label, so the number has to do the work for people who can’t tell the colours apart. The key lists the ranges for the same reason
  • People may ask "is 24 high?", since a score alone has no meaning until they learn the scale
  • The group lines (30, 20 and 8) are made up, and they decide who gets noticed
  • It uses the existing band colours, so people may still read red and orange as "act"

Ways to combine them

  • 4 and 5. Emphasise the top group, and show the drivers on every row. A leader sees where to look, and each role sees whether their part is involved.
  • 1 or 2, with 5. A neutral level label for the whole list, with the drivers underneath.
  • 3 and 6. A score bar for the whole list, with the leadership marker only where it is needed.
  • 6 now, 7 later. Start with the marker, and move to a role view if research shows several roles need different signals.
  • 8 and 5. The score styled by band, with the drivers underneath. No names for the groups, and each role still sees its own part. We add words once research tells us what people call the groups.
  • 8 first, words later. Test with only the styled score, ask users what they would call the groups and what they would group by, then choose from options 1 and 2 using their words.

Questions for the team

  • Who organises action? Is it only the CEO, or does it depend on the disease? This decides whether a marker for leadership (option 6) is right.
  • Who else needs to find their part, and how do they find it today?
  • Do we still want groups on the cards? The cards view groups diseases by priority action today. Options 1, 2 and 4 keep a form of grouping. Option 3 does not.
  • Where is the line for "top"? The 30 or more used here is made up, and sets who gets noticed.
  • Does the real scoring give us the drivers? Option 5 depends on being able to say which measure drives a score.

For research

The user researcher and the wider team decide how to test this. Things worth finding out:

  • Do people read a priority label as an instruction to them? Does the wording change that?
  • What would people call the groups of scores, if we gave them none? What would they group by? (Option 8 is built for this.)
  • What does a leader look for first, and does the signal find it?
  • Do people in other roles feel the page is talking to them when it is not, or miss something that is for them?
  • What would each role want the Priority column to tell them?
