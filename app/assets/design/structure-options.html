Disease-first or layer-first?

Users will start on the priority overview. From there, do they go into a disease and then see its layers (risk, impact, preparedness), or go into a layer and then see all diseases? Or do we need both?

All data is illustrative and the diseases are fictional. "Layer" means risk, impact or preparedness (control measures and response options).

Comparing the options

A: Disease-first

"What do I need to know about this disease?"

For

  • Priority is a score for each disease, so the overview and the next step line up.
  • One page answers "what is the so what, and what do I do?" (UN8).
  • Fits the workshop idea of showing risk, impact and preparedness together for a disease.
  • Adding preparedness later is another block on the page. The navigation doesn't change.
  • Headlines on the disease page give the "so what" for each layer without opening every layer page. They also keep the page short.

Against

  • Hard to compare one layer across diseases, e.g. "what changed in risk this month?" (UN2).
  • Layers come from different sources and dates, so each headline needs its own date and source.
  • Someone has to write or generate a headline for every layer and disease, and keep it current.

B: Layer-first

"How do all diseases compare on this layer?"

For

  • Matches how the evidence is produced: the IDM monthly report for risk, separate impact and preparedness data owned by different teams.
  • Easy to show "latest changes", sort and filter within one layer, with one scale and one colour meaning.
  • Each layer can launch when its data is ready.

Against

  • Priority is the combination. A layer-first route can feel disconnected from it.
  • No single page for one disease, so users do the joining up themselves.
  • Layers with no data yet (preparedness) show as empty tabs.

C: Both

"Let me start from either."

For

  • Serves both ways of thinking.
  • Only one destination page per disease and layer, so nothing is built twice.
  • The switch can start small, e.g. by disease only, and grow.

Against

  • More to design, build, test and keep consistent.
  • Users can lose track of where they are, so it needs clear labels and breadcrumbs.
  • Risk of two overviews that disagree on sort order.

Deciding

What the current wireframes already assume

The test round (risk and impact) leans both ways. Cards with a "Latest changes" tab (1B) behave like a layer page for risk. The detail pages (2A, 2B) behave like a disease page. Whichever way the team goes, the test should not mix the two without saying so.

Questions that decide it

  • Who are the users, and how do they work? Do SMEs think disease by disease, or does each own a layer (risk, impact, preparedness)?
  • What do they open the service to do? Act on the top priorities (disease-first), or review what changed this month (layer-first)?
  • How often does each layer update? Monthly for risk. Impact and preparedness are probably slower. Mixing them on one page could mislead.
  • Is the priority score the main product? If so, disease-first keeps it central.
  • Can we afford both? One destination page keeps the cost down.

A way forward to discuss

Start with disease-first as the default: priority overview, then a disease page with a headline from each layer, then the layer pages. Priority is per disease and the main question is "what do I act on?", and the headlines answer it without opening every layer. Add a light layer view on the overview, such as a layer selector above the same list, so users who want to compare one layer across diseases can. Both end on one disease + layer page (option C, built up in stages).

To check this with users, use a first-click test with two tasks: "Find which disease changed in risk this month" and "Find why this disease is top priority". Watch which route they take.

This is a suggestion for the team to challenge, not a decision. It depends on the answers above.
